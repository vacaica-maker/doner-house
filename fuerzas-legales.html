2.3 Political, Legal and Governmental Forces

David's Strategic Management describes governments as major regulators, employers and customers of every organization, so political, governmental and legal factors can represent key opportunities or threats regardless of firm size (Table 3-6). For a service business that prepares and sells food directly to the public, this force is unusually direct: sanitary and labeling regulation is not background context, it is the legal permission to operate at all. This page traces the specific regulatory framework the QSR industry must comply with to open and stay open in Bogotá.

2.3.1 Resolución 5109 de 2005: Labeling and Rotulation Requirements

1. Introduction

David's Table 3-6 lists "special local, state and federal laws" and "government regulations" among the key political/legal variables a firm must track. Resolución 5109 de 2005, issued by what was then the Ministry of Social Protection, is exactly this kind of variable for the food-service sector: it is the technical regulatory framework establishing mandatory labeling requirements for packaged food, packaging, and raw materials sold in Colombia. For any service business handling packaged goods, the practical effect is that every packaged item sold or repackaged must carry government-mandated information, not just a brand-chosen description.

2. Data

Mandatory Allergen Declarations (in force since 2005)

8

Allergen categories that must be explicitly declared (gluten cereals, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soy, milk, tree nuts), plus mandatory sulfite declaration (≥10 mg/kg)

  • 2005 — still in force today
    Resolución 5109 issued

    The Ministry of Social Protection established mandatory labeling requirements: specific food name, full ingredient list (highest to lowest proportion), batch/lot identification, expiration date, and storage instructions — unchanged and fully applicable as of 2026.

  • In force today
    8 allergen categories must be explicitly declared

    Gluten-containing cereals, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soy, milk, and tree nuts must be explicitly declared on any label — along with sulfites when present at 10 mg/kg or more — protecting consumers with food sensitivities (Art. 6, Resolución 5109/2005).

  • In force today
    Fractionated/repackaged food requires secondary labels

    Establishments that repackage food for resale must ensure secondary labels carry at minimum the food name, batch, and traceability data, under INVIMA oversight.

Mandatory label fields under Resolución 5109/2005

FieldRequirement
Food nameSpecific name reflecting the product's true nature — no misleading descriptions.
Ingredient listFull list of ingredients and additives, ordered from highest to lowest proportion.
Batch/lot identificationClear, traceable batch marking on every unit.
Expiration dateMarked visibly and indelibly; stickers to alter the date are expressly prohibited.
Storage instructionsConservation conditions needed to guarantee product safety.
Allergen declarationExplicit declaration of 8 categories: gluten cereals, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soy, milk, tree nuts — plus sulfites when present at ≥10 mg/kg.

3. Opportunities and Threats

Opportunity

  • Clear, fully compliant labeling — including transparent allergen declarations — builds consumer trust with the 88% of Colombian consumers who report being more health-conscious (see Social Forces), turning a legal requirement into a visible trust signal.

4. Analysis

Because Resolución 5109 has been in force since 2005, there is no ambiguity or transition period to plan around — compliant labeling is simply a fixed cost of doing business, best treated as a design decision made once at launch (menu boards, packaging, allergen call-outs) rather than an ongoing legal risk, as long as ingredient changes are tracked and reflected on labels going forward.

The six mandatory fields above break into two categories with different stakes: name, ingredients, batch and storage instructions are primarily traceability and quality-assurance requirements, enforced through INVIMA oversight of fractionated or repackaged food; the allergen declaration is the one field with direct consumer-safety and reputational consequences if omitted or wrong, which is why it also doubles as the strongest trust-building opportunity within an otherwise purely compliance-driven variable.

2.3.2 Resolución 2674 de 2013: Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP)

1. Introduction

Resolución 2674 de 2013 is the single most important legal pillar for Colombia's food industry — the government regulation, in David's Table 3-6 terms, that determines whether a food-service business can legally obtain INVIMA's inspection, surveillance, and control certificate at all. It is mandatory for every processing plant, point of sale, and fast-food service nationwide, covering building conditions, cold-chain control, pest management, and mandatory staff hygiene training.

2. Data

  • 2024611

Most Common Inspection Findings

  • Temperature record-keeping deficiencies (estimate)~40%

Industry-cited estimates; exact figures not confirmed in INVIMA, RedJurista, or Secretaría de Salud de Bogotá sources

  • 2013 — still in force today
    Resolución 2674 issued

    The Ministry of Health and Social Protection set nationwide Good Manufacturing Practice requirements. Under Art. 22, an establishment "may" apply a Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP) system "or another system that guarantees similar results" — an option among equivalent food-safety systems, not a HACCP mandate restricted to medium- or high-risk establishments. The resolution remains fully in force in 2026.

  • 2023→2024
    Foodborne illness outbreaks fall from 719 to 611

    Nationally reported ETA outbreaks declined year over year, though 2024's 611 outbreaks still affected 8,257 people, with Bogotá among the highest-incidence jurisdictions — and Bogotá closed more than 1,700 non-compliant establishments that same year.

  • In force today
    A large share of small establishments fail temperature record-keeping

    Industry estimates put temperature record-keeping and traceability deficiencies at close to 40% of small fast-food establishments, and non-compliant staff training (minimum 10 hrs/year required) at around 35% of findings — precise figures that could not be confirmed against a specific INVIMA, RedJurista, or Secretaría de Salud de Bogotá publication.

ComponentTechnical Requirement under Resolución 2674/2013
TemperatureMandatory cold-chain maintenance (refrigeration ≤4°C, freezing ≤-18°C) with verifiable daily records.
Water SupplyExclusive use of pressurized potable water, with semiannual physicochemical and microbiological analysis records.
Pest ManagementDocumented integrated pest-control program performed by certified companies (Art. 26); the resolution does not fix a minimum service frequency.
Sanitation PlanMandatory implementation of four basic programs: cleaning & disinfection, solid-waste disposal, pest control, and potable-water supply.

3. Opportunities and Threats

Threat

  • Compliant buildings, cold-chain monitoring, pest control, a documented food-safety system (HACCP or an equivalent), and mandatory staff training (10 hrs/year) are all upfront costs and ongoing obligations that must be absorbed before a food business can legally open — not costs that can be phased in later.

4. Analysis

With an estimated 40% of small establishments already failing temperature record-keeping, 35% of inspection findings tied to untrained staff, and 611 foodborne-illness outbreaks still reported nationally in 2024 despite the year-over-year decline, the compliance bar in this sector is clearly being missed by many existing small operators — which is exactly why it reads as a threat rather than an opportunity: it is a real, binding cost of entry, and the fact that competitors often skip it does not lower any operator's own obligation to meet it. Budgeting for compliant infrastructure and training from day one is the only way to avoid the food seizures and temporary closures that catch unprepared entrants.

The compliance table above splits into a fixed, one-time component (building materials, unidirectional process flow) and a recurring, operational component (cold-chain records, documented pest control, four sanitation programs, annual staff training). The fixed component is what a new entrant budgets for once at build-out; the recurring component is what most of the 40%/35% inspection failures actually come from, since it depends on daily staff discipline rather than a single upfront investment — which is precisely the gap digital traceability tools (Technological Forces 2.4.5) are built to close.
