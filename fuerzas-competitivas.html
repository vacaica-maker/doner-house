2.5 Competitive Forces

David's competitive-profile framework calls for a rigorous, side-by-side characterization of the direct competitors an industry entrant must be measured against. This section profiles the three main competitors identified in Bogotá's döner/shawarma niche: Gyros & Kebab, Shawarma Petra, and Filiák Cocina Griega.

2.5.1 Gyros & Kebab

1. Introduction

Gyros & Kebab operates as an independent, traditional establishment, concentrated mainly at its main location. It is an established, locally-run business in Bogotá's Middle Eastern food sector, but not a large chain, which makes it a useful reference point for how a mature, traditional-format competitor performs in this category.

2. Data

Strengths and Weaknesses (Beyond Price)

  • Strengths — Solid, traditional reputation for preparing classic Lebanese dishes and mezzes, building loyalty with a mature audience seeking authentic, traditional recipes.
  • Weaknesses — Traditional infrastructure and visual concept, less appealing to a youth/student audience; manual, slow operational processes during peak hours that hurt agility.
AttributeDetail
Price range$20K–$40K COP (some dishes and average consumption run higher)
Sales channelsModerate presence on last-mile aggregator apps and direct in-store sales, less focus on proprietary digital channels
Customer segmentOffice workers, area residents, and adult/traditional consumers with higher purchasing power
Geographic locationCarrera 13 # 82-28, a consolidated commercial and financial area of northern Bogotá

3. Opportunities and Threats

Opportunity

  • Some products are priced high for students or consumers looking for an affordable quick meal — we capitalize by offering Döner Kebab combos at accessible prices while keeping good quality and generous portions.

4. Analysis

Gyros & Kebab's traditional format and adult-skewing customer base mean it is not competing head-on for the student segment, even though its price range overlaps with a planned döner-kebab offer. Its weakness — slow, manual processes during peak hours — is exactly the kind of operational gap that digitally controlled kitchen equipment (Technological Forces 2.4.3) is designed to close, making speed a differentiator that plays directly against this specific competitor's soft spot.

2.5.2 Shawarma Petra

1. Introduction

Shawarma Petra is a specialized-format business present in high-flow gastronomic corridors, operating mainly from select strategic locations such as the Parque de la 93 area. Its specialization in shawarma and Middle Eastern fast food makes it the single most direct rival in this category.

2. Data

Strengths and Weaknesses (Beyond Price)

  • Strengths — A highly direct competitor due to its specialization in shawarma and Middle Eastern fast food; excellent location in high-end dining areas and fast in-person service.
  • Weaknesses — Elevated pricing driven by the high fixed costs of its prime locations; little conceptual differentiation in its brand identity versus other fast-food players.
AttributeDetail
Price range$20K–$40K COP
Sales channelsHigh digital integration with last-mile platforms (Rappi and its own delivery)
Customer segmentTourists, executives, young professionals, and nightlife/commercial-area visitors
Geographic locationParque de la 93 area (Cra. 13 # 93-85) and points of direct influence in northern Bogotá

3. Opportunities and Threats

Threat

  • It is the most direct rival in the category (same shawarma/Döner-style product), with a prime location and strong Rappi/own-delivery integration already capturing the same customer occasion.

4. Analysis

Shawarma Petra is the benchmark rival across this whole competitive analysis (Section 2.6.1 treats it as the highest-intensity rivalry relationship) precisely because it shares both product category and delivery-channel strategy. Its own weakness — little conceptual differentiation — is the specific gap a clearly German-identity, döner-specialized concept is positioned to exploit, rather than competing purely on the price range the two already share.

2.5.3 Filiák Cocina Griega

1. Introduction

Filiák Cocina Griega is a business with structured growth, operating two strategic locations in Bogotá and establishing itself as a Mediterranean/Greek cuisine brand with recognition in medium-high and high-income areas — the strongest brand and digital-presence competitor of the three.

2. Data

Strengths and Weaknesses (Beyond Price)

  • Strengths — Excellent digital reputation and outstanding ratings (4.8 on platforms); modern architectural concepts (such as shipping-container or open-air spaces in Zona G and Parque de la 93) and strong social-media presence.
  • Weaknesses — An excessively broad menu that dilutes fast-food specialization; long preparation times and high prices for an economical, quick-consumption category.
AttributeDetail
Price range$28K–$44K COP, depending on product/combo
Sales channelsHeavily digitized, active on aggregators (DiDi Food, Rappi) and own reservation/online-ordering channels
Customer segmentYoung professionals, high-purchasing-power university students, couples, urban foodies
Geographic locationZona G (Calle 68 # 6-39, Casa Sexta) and Plazoleta Jerre Jerre (Calle 93B # 13-65)

3. Opportunities and Threats

Threat

  • Its 4.8 online rating and strong social-media presence give it an outsized digital-discovery advantage that a brand-new business will need time to match.

4. Analysis

Filiák's broad, high-price menu targets a different occasion (a higher-spend, Instagram-worthy dining experience) than a fast, accessible döner-kebab combo, so the two are only partial substitutes despite sharing the higher end of the price band. Its real competitive weight is not pricing pressure but the digital-reputation gap: a 4.8 rating built over multiple locations is not something a new entrant can close quickly, which is why execution quality from day one (Competitive Forces 2.6.5) matters more against this specific rival than product positioning does.

5. Bibliography

Source Filiák Cocina Griega — Locales DiDi Food — Filiák Cocina Griega, Chapinero

Conclusion

Our core strategy will be to differentiate through accessible pricing, speed, a specialized menu, and a clear German identity. Rather than competing on price alone, we aim to offer a different, appealing experience for young people, students, and anyone wanting to try an alternative to traditional fast food.

2.6 Competitive Analysis: Porter's Five-Forces Model

2.6.1 Rivalry among Competing Firms

1. Introduction

David's five-forces model treats rivalry among existing firms as usually the most powerful of the five forces, because it is the one competitors actively manage day to day. For a service business, rivalry intensity is set less by industry-wide statistics than by how the specific local competitors behave — which is why this variable is analyzed directly through the same three competitors profiled in section 2.5.

2. Data

  • Cra 13 #82-28
    Gyros & Kebab — adjacent segment

    Established, traditional operator targeting office workers and adult consumers; price range $20K–$40K COP.

  • Parque de la 93
    Shawarma Petra — most direct rival

    Same product category, prime location, strong Rappi/own-delivery integration; price range $20K–$40K COP.

  • Zona G & Cl. 93B
    Filiák Cocina Griega — brand/digital leader

    4.8 online rating, two locations, strongest brand and social-media presence; price range $28K–$44K COP.

Rivalry profile at a glance

CompetitorRivalry IntensitySegment Overlap
Gyros & KebabModerateAdjacent (adult/office-worker audience)
Shawarma PetraHighDirect (same product category)
Filiák Cocina GriegaModerateAdjacent (higher-income foodie audience)

3. Opportunities and Threats

Opportunity

  • None of the three incumbents has locked in strong brand loyalty among students, leaving an opening for a sharply differentiated new entrant to win that specific segment.

4. Analysis

Rivalry intensity is high: all three competitors are established and price bands overlap heavily with the pricing a new entrant in this category would plan around. But high rivalry does not mean a locked market — because none of the three has built loyalty with students specifically, the practical strategy for a new entrant is to compete on speed, specialization, and identity for that underserved segment rather than attacking any of the three head-on for their existing customer base.

Only one of the three (Shawarma Petra) is rated a direct, high-intensity rival; the other two sit in adjacent segments where rivalry is real but less acute. This asymmetry means overall rivalry intensity, while high in aggregate, is concentrated in a single competitor relationship rather than spread evenly across all three, which should focus competitive-response planning on Shawarma Petra specifically rather than treating all three incumbents as equally urgent threats.

2.6.2 Potential Entry of New Competitors

1. Introduction

David identifies barriers to entry — capital requirements, economies of scale, brand loyalty, and government policy — as the core determinants of this force. For a service business, entry barriers are usually lower than in manufacturing, which is exactly the case here: the threat of entry into Bogotá's specialized döner niche combines a genuinely low operational barrier with financial and regulatory filters that still meaningfully protect formalized businesses.

3. Opportunities and Threats

Opportunity

  • The Secretaría Distrital de Salud formalization process and $100M COP capital requirement discourage informal, undercapitalized entrants, protecting whoever enters properly formalized and well-capitalized.

4. Analysis

The net entry barrier is moderate rather than high: capital and regulatory requirements filter out informal, undercapitalized entrants (the opportunity), but zero brand-switching cost means any second well-capitalized entrant faces no more friction than the first one did. The strategic implication for the industry is that formalization and capital adequacy protect a properly funded entrant from informal competition, but not from another equally well-funded rival — so speed to market and brand-building matter as much as meeting the capital threshold.

The four barriers in the table above are not equally durable: capital and regulatory filters are one-time hurdles cleared at entry, while the absence of brand loyalty is a permanent structural feature of this category that keeps the market open to further entry indefinitely. This means the entry barrier this industry presents is front-loaded — hard to clear once, but offering no lasting protection afterward — so any advantage gained at launch must be actively defended through ongoing execution rather than assumed to persist.

2.6.3 Potential Development of Substitute Products

1. Introduction

David defines substitutes as products from a different category that satisfy the same customer need, which for a service business is often the most underestimated of the five forces because substitutes rarely look like direct competitors. In this category, the relevant substitutes are any other immediate-consumption food option a budget-conscious Bogotá consumer could choose instead.

3. Opportunities and Threats

Threat

  • Cheaper, instantly available substitutes like arepas ($10K–$18K) sit well below Döner Kebab's planned price, and any wait over 10–15 minutes — the documented patience threshold for a hungry student — sends budget-conscious students straight to them.

4. Analysis

The threat of substitution in this category is concentrated in one variable an operator can actually control: speed. Price alone does not explain substitute risk, since a planned döner-kebab price already sits close to chain-hamburger combos; it is the 10–15 minute patience threshold that determines whether a price-comparable customer stays or switches to an instantly available cheaper option. Operational speed is therefore the single highest-leverage defense against this force, ahead of price positioning.

The threat-level ranking above shows the highest-risk substitute (arepas) is also the cheapest and fastest, meaning it competes on both dimensions a döner-kebab operator cannot easily match on price alone. The moderate-threat substitutes, by contrast, are closer in price but not necessarily faster, which is the gap a speed-focused operational strategy is specifically designed to exploit.

2.6.4 Bargaining Power of Suppliers

1. Introduction

David notes supplier power rises when few suppliers exist and switching between them is costly — a condition that varies input by input rather than applying uniformly to a whole industry. In the döner-kebab category, the three main input categories sit at very different points on that scale.

    National processors (e.g. Friko) and Corabastos wholesale distributors allow volume negotiation within the $100M COP budget.

  • Low power
    Pita bread: high density of local bakeries

    A dense local artisan/industrial bakery network in Bogotá keeps this input highly substitutable.

  • High power
    Vertical rotisseries: a handful of exclusive importers

    High-end, low-energy rotisseries are sold by very few specialized distributors, who control warranty, maintenance and spare parts.

Pita breadLowHigh density of local artisan/industrial bakeries in Bogotá.

3. Opportunities and Threats

Threat

  • The one critical piece of equipment — the vertical rotisserie — sits with a handful of exclusive importers, so any pricing, warranty or repair-timeline leverage on that item rests with the supplier, not the operator.

4. Analysis

Overall supplier power is low-to-moderate across the supply chain, but concentrated risk sits entirely in one input: the rotisserie. Because meat and bread suppliers are plentiful and easily switched, the practical priority is not diversifying those (already low-risk) categories, but negotiating service/warranty terms and holding spare parts for the rotisserie specifically, since it is the one supplier relationship an operator cannot easily replace mid-operation.

2.6.5 Bargaining Power of Consumers

1. Introduction

David identifies buyer power as high when switching costs are low and buyers are price sensitive — and for a delivery-app-mediated service business, that power is further amplified by pure price/quality transparency, since the customer can compare every option on one screen before choosing.

2. Data

Price-Sensitive Consumers

Of consumers are highly price-sensitive

Switching Cost

$0

Cost for a customer to switch to a competitor the very next purchase

  • Reaffirming Social Forces
    49% of consumers are highly price-sensitive

    Consistent with the price-sensitivity data already established under Social Forces 2.2.2, seeking the best cost-benefit ratio amid inflation.

  • Structural
    Zero switching cost

    Bogotá's dining scene offers fast-food alternatives on every corner, removing any barrier to switching brands from one purchase to the next.

  • Reaffirming Technological Forces
    Delivery apps amplify buyer power

    As established in Technological Forces 2.4.1, aggregators let a customer compare prices, ratings and delivery times across every nearby competitor on one screen.

Sources of buyer power

FactorEffect on Buyer Power
Price sensitivity (49%)Pushes consumers to actively seek the best cost-benefit ratio rather than defaulting to habit.
Switching cost ($0)Removes any friction that would otherwise lock a customer into one brand.
Delivery-app transparencyLets buyers compare price, rating and delivery time across every nearby competitor instantly.

3. Opportunities and Threats

Threat

  • With zero switching cost and every competitor visible side-by-side on delivery apps, a single bad review or slow delivery can send a customer to a rival on their very next order.

4. Analysis

Buyer power here is high on every dimension David identifies: price sensitivity, zero switching cost, and full information transparency. Because this power cannot be reduced structurally — no single operator can make substitutes disappear or lock in switching costs in this category — the only lever available is consistently strong execution — rating and review management become a core operating discipline, not a marketing afterthought.

The three factors in the table above compound rather than act independently: transparency alone would matter less without price sensitivity to act on it, and price sensitivity alone would matter less without zero switching cost to enable acting on it. It is the combination of all three that makes buyer power in this category structurally high rather than a temporary market condition, which is why execution quality has to be treated as a permanent competitive requirement, not a launch-phase priority that eases once the business is established.
