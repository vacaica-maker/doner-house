1. Gyros & Kebab

Size and Presence

  • Operates as an independent, traditional establishment, concentrated mainly at its main location. It is an established, locally-run business in Bogotá's Middle Eastern food sector, but not a large chain.

Strengths and Weaknesses (Beyond Price)

  • Strengths — Solid, traditional reputation for preparing classic Lebanese dishes and mezzes, building loyalty with a mature audience seeking authentic, traditional recipes.
  • Weaknesses — Traditional infrastructure and visual concept, less appealing to a youth/student audience; manual, slow operational processes during peak hours that hurt agility.

Sales Channels

  • Moderate presence on last-mile aggregator apps and direct in-store sales, with less focus on proprietary digital channels (app/web).

Customer Segment

  • Targets office workers, area residents, and adult/traditional consumers with higher purchasing power, away from the mass student segment.

Geographic Location

  • Located at Carrera 13 # 82-28 (a consolidated commercial and financial area of northern Bogotá).

Opportunities

  • Some products are priced high for students or consumers looking for an affordable quick meal — we capitalize by offering Döner Kebab combos at accessible prices while keeping good quality and generous portions.

Threats

  • Its solid, traditional reputation and established local trajectory could still pull in adjacent, price-insensitive customers Döner House would otherwise reach as it grows beyond the student segment.
Source Queresto — Gyros y Kebab Degusta — Gyros y Kebab

2. Shawarma Petra

Size and Presence

  • A specialized-format business present in high-flow gastronomic corridors, operating mainly from select strategic locations (such as the Parque de la 93 area).

Strengths and Weaknesses (Beyond Price)

  • Strengths — A highly direct competitor due to its specialization in shawarma and Middle Eastern fast food; excellent location in high-end dining areas and fast in-person service.
  • Weaknesses — Elevated pricing driven by the high fixed costs of its prime locations; little conceptual differentiation in its brand identity versus other fast-food players.

Sales Channels

  • High digital integration with last-mile platforms (Rappi and its own delivery), optimizing delivery within its area of influence.

Customer Segment

  • Tourists, executives, young professionals, and visitors to the nightlife and commercial areas of northern Bogotá.

Geographic Location

  • Located in the Parque de la 93 area (Cra. 13 # 93-85) and points of direct influence in northern Bogotá.

Opportunities

  • Its lack of conceptual differentiation is an opening — we capitalize by differentiating through a German identity, a youthful image, and a concept specialized in Döner Kebab rather than competing on price alone.

Threats

  • It is the most direct rival in the category (same shawarma/Döner-style product), with a prime location and strong Rappi/own-delivery integration already capturing the same customer occasion.
Source RestaurantGuru — Shawarma Petra Bogotá

3. Filiák Cocina Griega

Size and Presence

  • A business with structured growth, with two strategic locations in Bogotá, establishing itself as a Mediterranean/Greek cuisine brand with recognition in medium-high and high-income areas.

Strengths and Weaknesses (Beyond Price)

  • Strengths — Excellent digital reputation and outstanding ratings (4.8 on platforms); modern architectural concepts (such as shipping-container or open-air spaces in Zona G and Parque de la 93) and strong social-media presence.
  • Weaknesses — An excessively broad menu that dilutes fast-food specialization; long preparation times and high prices for an economical, quick-consumption category.

Sales Channels

  • Heavily digitized, with active presence on aggregators (DiDi Food, Rappi) and its own reservation/online-ordering channels.

Customer Segment

  • Young professionals, high-purchasing-power university students, couples, and urban foodies looking for Instagram-worthy dining experiences.

Geographic Location

  • Two key locations: one in Zona G (Calle 68 # 6-39, Casa Sexta) and another at Plazoleta Jerre Jerre (Calle 93B # 13-65).

Opportunities

  • Some combos are priced high and its menu is broader — we capitalize by offering a simpler, more specialized menu, enabling more accessible prices and faster service.

Threats

  • Its 4.8 online rating and strong social-media presence give it an outsized digital-discovery advantage that a brand-new business will need time to match.
Source Filiák Cocina Griega — Locales DiDi Food — Filiák Cocina Griega, Chapinero

Conclusion

Our core strategy will be to differentiate through accessible pricing, speed, a specialized menu, and a clear German identity. Rather than competing on price alone, we aim to offer a different, appealing experience for young people, students, and anyone wanting to try an alternative to traditional fast food.

2.6 Competitive Analysis: Porter's Five-Forces Model

2.6.1 Rivalry among Competing Firms

Rivalry is analyzed through the same three direct competitors profiled in section 2.5 above — Gyros & Kebab, Shawarma Petra, and Filiák Cocina Griega. Shawarma Petra represents the most direct rivalry (same product category), while Gyros & Kebab and Filiák target adjacent but distinct customer segments. Overall rivalry intensity is high: all three competitors are well established in their niches, price bands overlap heavily with Döner House's planned pricing, and none has strong brand loyalty locking in the youth/student segment — leaving room for Döner House to compete on speed, specialization, and identity rather than price alone.

Opportunities

  • None of the three incumbents has locked in strong brand loyalty among students, leaving an opening for a sharply differentiated new entrant to win that segment.

Threats

  • All three rivals are already established and operating profitably in overlapping price bands, so Döner House enters an already-contested market rather than an empty one.

2.6.2 Potential Entry of New Competitors

The threat of entry into Bogotá's specialized fast-food (Döner Kebab) niche combines a low operational barrier with very clear financial and regulatory filters that protect formalized businesses:

Initial Investment Structure (Budget of 100 Million COP)

  • Launching a small physical counter-format store or an optimized dark kitchen in Bogotá requires an initial investment of approximately $100,000,000 COP.
  • This capital is distributed strategically across three critical areas: 40% for infrastructure fit-out and legally required sanitary finishes; 30% for specialized machinery (vertical rotisserie, extraction hoods, refrigeration, and POS); and 30% for operating working capital to ensure sustainability during the first 4 to 6 months of launch, the period when sales volume stabilizes. Current MSME credit conditions in the country make leverage more expensive, requiring rigorous management of initial cash flow.

Regulatory Barrier and Sanitary Burden (INVIMA / Health Secretariats)

  • Formalization before Colombia's sanitary control entities is an unavoidable filter of time and fixed costs. Obtaining sanitary clearance and complying with Resolución 2674 de 2013 takes on average between 2 and 4 months of paperwork, physical fit-out of washable finishes, and food-handling certifications. This discourages fully informal, small-scale competitors, but requires a new business to set aside specific upfront resources to ensure legal compliance from day one.

Nonexistent Brand Loyalty and Low Switching Costs

  • In the urban fast-food segment, consumers (especially young people and university students) show no entrenched loyalty to a döner or shawarma brand. The switching cost is zero: if a location is too slow or raises its prices, the customer switches on their very next purchase. This makes it easier for new competitors to enter, but requires the business to differentiate through a very strong brand identity and service speed to retain customers.

Technological Access to Kitchen Equipment (Vertical Rotisseries)

  • The business's core depends on vertical rotisseries. While local machinery distributors exist in Bogotá's commercial areas, imported equipment with high energy efficiency and durability involves longer import lead times or higher costs, which limits improvised entry by competitors who lack planned initial investment capital.

Opportunities

  • The 2–4 month INVIMA formalization process and $100M COP capital requirement discourage informal, undercapitalized entrants, protecting whoever enters properly formalized and well-capitalized.

Threats

  • Zero brand-switching cost for consumers means the same low barrier that lets Döner House enter also lets the next competitor enter just as easily.
Source Portafolio — Cuánto Cuesta Montar un Restaurante en Colombia Cámara de Comercio de Bogotá — Crea tu Empresa

2.6.3 Potential Development of Substitute Products

Bogotá's urban fast-food consumer faces a very wide range of immediate-consumption alternatives that compete directly for their daily budget:

Substitute Map and Price Ranges

  • Döner Kebab competes against an established range of affordable substitutes: artisanal filled arepas ($10,000–$18,000 COP), quick-format fried chicken pieces, pizza by the slice, and combo meals at mass commercial hamburger chains ($25,000–$35,000 COP).
  • At a projected price of $20,000 to $40,000 COP, döner sits at a competitive equilibrium point: it offers a perception of higher nutritional value and freshness than an industrial hamburger or a traditional arepa, but competes in the same affordability range for students and office workers.

Student Behavior Under Time and Budget Constraints

  • Facing the urgency of curbing hunger between classes or work shifts, the average student weighs a price-time-satiety equation. If the wait time at the location exceeds 10 or 15 minutes, the consumer will immediately opt for an instantly available substitute (such as an empanada or a chain hamburger), which shows that operational speed is the best defense against substitutes.

Opportunities

  • Döner Kebab's perceived freshness and higher nutritional value versus an industrial hamburger or arepa let it justify a comparable price while still standing out from cheaper substitutes.

Threats

  • Cheaper, instantly available substitutes like arepas ($10K–$18K) sit well below Döner Kebab's planned price, and any wait over 10–15 minutes sends budget-conscious students straight to them.
Source Fenalco — Tendencias de Consumo Masivo y Gasto en Alimentos Fuera del Hogar La República — El Mapa de la Comida Rápida en Colombia

2.6.4 Bargaining Power of Suppliers

Meat and Specialized Protein Supply

  • Sourcing meat (chicken, beef, and lamb for the döner spit) relies on a mixed network: large national industrial processors (such as Friko) and institutional distributors at wholesale markets like Corabastos. Because multiple certified slaughter-plant options exist, the bargaining power of any single meat supplier is moderate, allowing volume price negotiation within the $100 million budget.

Bakery Input Suppliers (Pita / Arabic Bread)

  • Pita bread production benefits from a high density of local artisan and industrial bakeries in Bogotá supplying fresh bread daily. This gives the venture significant freedom of choice, minimizing bread manufacturers' bargaining power.

Specialized Machinery and Parts (Vertical Rotisserie)

  • High-end, low-energy-consumption vertical rotisseries are typically imported or sold by a very small group of specialized distributors of industrial gastronomic equipment in the country. On this specific component, suppliers have high bargaining power, since warranties, technical maintenance, and spare parts depend on exclusive channels.

Opportunities

  • The high density of meat processors and local bakeries in Bogotá means most of the supply chain can be multi-sourced, keeping input costs and terms negotiable.

Threats

  • The one critical piece of equipment — the vertical rotisserie — sits with a handful of exclusive importers, so any pricing, warranty or repair-timeline leverage on that item rests with the supplier, not Döner House.

2.6.5 Bargaining Power of Consumers

Extreme Price Sensitivity

  • Reaffirming the social data on the Colombian market, 49% of consumers are highly price-sensitive, actively seeking the best cost-benefit ratio amid inflation and contracting household spending.

Zero Switching Cost and Market Saturation

  • The customer holds very high bargaining power because switching costs are nonexistent: Bogotá's dining scene offers fast-food alternatives on every corner, eliminating any barrier for a buyer to switch brands from one day to the next.

The Amplifying Role of Delivery Apps (Rappi)

  • As established under Technological Forces, last-mile platforms amplify consumer power to the extreme. By allowing instant, side-by-side comparison of prices, ratings, delivery times, and photos across dozens of direct competitors on a single screen, the customer exerts absolute control over the market. This forces the new venture to manage its online reputation impeccably, offer attractive promotions, and maintain a rigorous quality standard to counter the digital buyer's high demands.

Opportunities

  • A high-transparency market rewards genuinely good value: consistent quality and fair pricing can convert price-driven, delivery-app-comparing consumers into repeat customers faster than in a low-transparency market.

Threats

  • With zero switching cost and every competitor visible on the same screen, a single bad review or slow delivery can send a customer to a rival on their very next order.
Source Fedesarrollo — Impacto de la Economía Digital y Plataformas de Entrega en Colombia Ipsos Colombia — Tendencias del Consumidor y Hábitos de Compra
