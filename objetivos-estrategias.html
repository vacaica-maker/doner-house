4. Establish Long-Term Objectives

How Döner Street's long-term and annual objectives, execution timeline, Balanced Scorecard and strategy choices tie together — most of the groundwork already lives inside the Internal Audit, linked below section by section.

← Back to Index

4.1 Long-Term Objectives

David defines long-term objectives as the specific results a firm expects to achieve by pursuing its strategies, usually set two to five years out and covering areas such as profitability, growth, market share, and the quality or reach of the product line. For Döner Street, that two-to-five-year horizon is already built out as the “Target (Dec 31, 2027)” column across the four Balanced Scorecard perspectives, rather than as a separate standalone list.

Our long-term objectives cover two years: 2026 (Year 1, opening) and 2027 (Year 2), the minimum horizon David recommends (two to five years). The targets are set for 31 December 2027 against the 31 December 2026 baseline. Following David, every objective is quantitative, measurable, realistic, understandable, challenging, hierarchical, obtainable, congruent across areas, and has a deadline.

See the Target (Dec 31, 2027) column in 4.4.1 Financial Perspective, 4.4.2 Customer Perspective, 4.4.3 Internal Business Process Perspective and 4.4.4 Learning & Growth Perspective below.

4.2 Annual Objectives

Annual objectives are the short-term, decentralized milestones that each position or functional area is accountable for within a given year — the operational bridge between a long-term target and the day-to-day work of the people who have to deliver it. For Döner Street, that bridge is built at the position level: three SMART objectives each for the Store Manager and the Store Associates, covering sales growth, customer satisfaction, food waste, cross-training, stockouts and order-fulfillment speed. Following David (Ch. 7), each annual objective below is measurable, consistent with the Balanced Scorecard targets in 4.4, reasonable, challenging, clear, has a deadline, and carries a reward or a sanction.

4.4 Balanced Scorecard

Kaplan & Norton’s Balanced Scorecard translates strategy into four linked perspectives — Financial, Customer, Internal Business Process, and Learning & Growth — each with its own objectives, measures, 2027 targets and initiatives. The perspectives cascade bottom-up: Learning & Growth builds the people and systems capability that Internal Business Process turns into operational performance, which drives the Customer outcomes that ultimately produce the Financial results. Every objective below is grounded in a section already developed in the Internal Audit, not invented for this scorecard, so the targets stay internally consistent with the Marketing, Production, MIS and Management sections they reference.

Book guidelines used. Every objective below was checked against David’s eight criteria for long-term objectives (Ch. 5, see 4.1) and written in Kaplan & Norton’s format (Ch. 9, Table 9-6): a measurable KPI, a target with a deadline, and an owner. Each table below reads left to right as one sentence — Objective, then the KPI that measures it, then the target, then the initiative that gets us there — with the baseline, check frequency and responsible person as supporting detail under the KPI.

3. Why we chose these objectives

Objective 1. Cash covers salaries, suppliers and taxes in the low and dead seasons, including the COP 16.1M of 2026 taxes payable and the 8.5% payroll increase in 2027. The 2027 projection gives COP 172.6M of cash; the COP 160M target leaves a 7% safety margin. Moving money to the savings account does not increase total cash, so the reserve has its own KPI.

Objective 2. Ingredients are the largest cost (55.4% of gross sales), so food cost is the main ROA driver. With food cost at 53.5% the projection gives net income of COP 43.0M and ROA of 18.8%. If food cost stays at 55.4%, net income falls to about COP 26.8M and ROA to about 13.2%. No new owner money is needed.

Objective 3. Only 13.1% of assets are financed by liabilities, all short-term and none from banks. The projection gives a debt ratio of 15.8% and a current ratio of 4.9x in 2027. The 20% ceiling leaves room for higher payroll and tax liabilities but not for new borrowing.

How the Four Perspectives Fit Together

Read bottom-up, the cascade is: Learning & Growth’s cross-training and MIS reliability objectives are what make the Internal Process perspective’s speed, compliance and waste targets achievable in practice rather than aspirational; the Internal Process perspective’s speed and quality control is what the Customer perspective’s service-guarantee and reputation objectives are actually promising to customers; and the Customer perspective’s pricing tolerance, loyalty behavior and reputation are what make the Financial perspective’s liquidity, ROA and capital-structure targets reachable without external financing. No objective in this scorecard sits in only one perspective — the stamp-card loyalty program, the outgoing Quality Check, and the MIS data backbone each appear as the mechanism in one perspective and the measured outcome in another, which is the check that the four perspectives were built together rather than independently.

4.5 Financial Objectives and Strategic Objectives

David separates the two kinds of result a firm's objectives can point to: financial objectives, tied directly to profitability and capital structure (revenue growth, return on investment, dividend, liquidity), and strategic objectives, tied to competitive position (market share, product quality, customer satisfaction, innovation). Both already exist for Döner Street inside the Balanced Scorecard: the three Financial Perspective objectives are the financial objectives, and the Customer, Internal Process and Learning & Growth objectives are the strategic objectives.

Financial Objectives 1–3 (liquidity, ROA, conservative capital structure) are in 4.4.1 Financial Perspective; the Strategic Objectives are in 4.4.2 Customer, 4.4.3 Internal Business Process and 4.4.4 Learning & Growth, right above.

Following David, the financial objectives (liquidity, ROA, capital structure) and the strategic objectives (speed, reputation, loyalty, process quality, people) all have a measure, a deadline and an owner. The financial targets come from one 2027 projection that balances (assets COP 228.9M = liabilities COP 36.1M + equity COP 192.8M), so they are realistic and congruent with each other. The book also warns about trade-offs: the 7% price increase helps short-term results, so the Type 5 KPIs in 4.8 check that customers still see the offer as the best value.

4.6 Type of Strategy

David groups the strategies available to a firm into broad families — integration, intensive, diversification and defensive — each suited to a different competitive situation. Two families apply directly to Döner Street at this stage: Intensive Strategies (market penetration, market development, product development) and Diversification Strategies (related and unrelated), both developed in full below.

4.6.1 Intensive Strategies

Intensive strategies require intensive effort to improve a firm's competitive position with existing products, before reaching for a new product or a new business altogether. David groups three under this family — market penetration, market development and product development.

1. Market Penetration

Strategy: sell more to the same market — students and young people already inside the Calle 72 / Autopista Norte corridor — without changing the product or the location.

This is the strategy David reserves for firms that do not change market or add anything new to reach a different target market — that is Market Development, the next strategy below. Here the customer, the product and the store stay exactly the same; only two levers move: more orders per existing customer, and a bigger share of each order. Döner Street pulls both with pricing and the upsell/loyalty mechanics already built into the Financial and Customer scorecards (4.4.1, 4.4.2), rather than inventing new, unbudgeted tactics.

Actions:

  • “Student Combo” at COP 25,900 with student ID, Monday to Thursday, 2–5 PM — the dead hours between lunch and dinner — to add about 30 combos a day without adding capacity.
  • POS upsell script on every Medium Combo (“Upgrade to Large?”), targeting 11.1% of Medium Combo orders (4.4.1), so the same customer spends more per visit.
  • Stamp card (5 purchases = free Large Combo upgrade) to convert first-time visitors into repeat ones, measured by the ≥ 30% reorder rate (Customer Objective 3).
  • 2x1 Tuesday, funded from 50% of the Régimen SIMPLE tax savings, to raise weekday visit frequency without discounting the regular menu.

Objective: grow gross sales to ≥ COP 1,309M by 31 Dec 2027 (+8% vs. 2026, Annual Objective 4.2) from the current customer base, with no new target market, no new store and no new product line.

Book guidelines: the market is not saturated (44% have never heard of döner), and usage can grow (47% eat fast food weekly or more). Planned marketing returns are high (revenue multiple 3.75x, 3.4.5), but there is no sales history yet. Recommended as the main growth strategy.

2. Market Development

Strategy: reach customers the single store cannot reach on foot, through new channels and nearby corporate demand — not through a second location.

A new store in Cajíca or elsewhere would need capital and a second Store Manager Döner Street does not have yet (IFE weakness #5, 3.3.2); the book guideline for market development also requires the brand to be “very successful” first (4.6.1 guidelines below). So for 2027, geographic reach grows only through channels that use the existing kitchen and staff.

Actions:

  • List on Rappi and DiDi Food within a 3 km delivery radius, with orders ready in 10 minutes (the same promise as dine-in), to reach 20% of sales through delivery within 6 months.
  • Promote the own WhatsApp ordering channel, priced 8% below the delivery apps, to move 15% of all orders off the 18%–30% app commission by December 2027.
  • Sell corporate lunch packs by WhatsApp to offices on the corridor, targeting 25 packs a week in the low-season months (January, June, July, December) when the kitchen has spare capacity.
  • Hold any new physical location for a 2028 decision, after the first full year of reviews, reputation and ROA data (4.4.4, “How we measure and correct”) shows the concept travels.

Objective: reach 20% of sales through delivery and 15% through the own channel by 31 Dec 2027, without adding a second kitchen, a second lease or new debt.

Book guidelines: delivery apps are reliable new channels and there is spare capacity on low-season days. But the brand is not yet “very successful” and capital and managers are limited. Only new delivery and WhatsApp channels in 2027; a second location is a 2028 decision.

3. Product Development

Strategy: add variety around the existing döner-only menu without turning Döner Street into a different, broader-menu concept.

Taste (64%) and ingredient quality (56%) are the top purchase factors in the survey (3.4.1), ahead of price (43%), so new items are judged first on whether they protect that identity — not just whether they are easy to make. With R&D limited to the Store Manager and no proprietary recipe to defend (IFE weakness #2), every new item ships as a time-boxed test before it becomes a permanent SKU.

Actions:

  • Develop 2 signature house sauces with local suppliers and test them with at least 100 customers; register the winning name and recipe (ties to the SWOT Matrix, WO Strategy 2, 5.2.1).
  • Run a spicy Döner variant and a customizable Döner bowl as 4-week limited-time specials, one at a time, so demand for each can be read cleanly in the POS.
  • Keep a specialty item on the permanent menu only if it sells at ≥ 10% of daily transactions during its 4-week test and holds the 35% target margin; drop it otherwise.
  • Collect a short in-store and WhatsApp survey during each test window instead of guessing demand from social-media engagement.

Objective: add at most one proven item to the permanent menu in 2027, selected only from items that clear the 10%-of-transactions bar in their test window, so the menu stays small enough to protect the 600-second production line (3.4.2, 3.6.1).

Book guidelines: rivals offer good quality at similar prices (Filiák). But the product is not mature, technology changes slowly and R&D is small. Test as limited-time specials only, and only add what the 10% sell-through bar proves customers actually want.

4.6.2 Diversification Strategies

Diversification means adding a new product, service or business beyond the firm's current one. David splits it in two: related diversification adds a new business that shares meaningful value-chain fit with the existing one (suppliers, production know-how, brand, distribution), while unrelated diversification adds a new business with little or no operational overlap, usually chosen to spread risk or put idle capital to work somewhere the core business can't reach. Döner Street is a single, equity-funded, founder-led store (3.3, 3.5.2) with an IFE score of 2.51, just above average (3.9) — any diversification has to be judged against that limited capacity, not against what a larger chain could absorb.

Related Diversification — “Döner Street at Home” Retail Grab-and-Go Line

Strategy: package the same sliced döner meat, house sauces and pita already produced for the store into a chilled “assemble-at-home” kit, sold through grocery and convenience outlets along the Calle 72 / Metro Line 1 corridor and through Döner Street's own ordering channel.

Why it's related: the kit uses the same meat and pita suppliers that already keep input costs and supplier power low (2.6.4), the same house sauces and toppings the survey identifies as the most-wanted add-ons — fries 57%, cheese 53%, house sauces 51% (3.4.1) — and the same incoming/outgoing Quality Check discipline that is already one of Döner Street's strongest IFE factors (3.6.1, 3.9). It is a new product and a new channel, but no new industry, recipe, or supplier relationship.

What it's for: it buys the brand shelf-level visibility beyond a single store's four walls, which helps offset IFE weakness #1, “unknown brand with no reviews yet” (3.9), while leaning on freshness and execution rather than a proprietary recipe Döner Street doesn't have (IFE weakness #2).

Main risk: cold-chain retail distribution adds food-safety and logistics complexity on top of a single Store Manager with no assistant (IFE weakness #5, 3.3.2) — this only works if it doesn't pull attention away from the ≈10-minute kitchen-line standard the core store is built around (3.6.1).

Book guidelines: related products could balance seasonal valleys. But the industry is growing (not slow) and the management team is one Store Manager. Not in 2027; review in 2028.

Unrelated Diversification — “Corridor Capital” Automated Micro-Retail Kiosks

Strategy: use owner equity to deploy a small number of unattended vending / micro-retail kiosks (snacks, drinks, phone charging) at the new Metro Line 1 and TransMilenio station on the Calle 72 corridor, run as a separate business with its own P&L, independent of the restaurant.

Why it's unrelated: there is no shared recipe, kitchen process, supplier or customer relationship with the döner business — it is a vending / retail-technology operation (equipment leasing, cash and card collection, remote maintenance), chosen only because it sits on the same physical corridor Döner Street already knows is under redevelopment and has built-in commuter traffic none of the three rivals currently captures (2.2.1, IFE strength #2).

What it's for: Döner Street is 100% equity-funded with no external line of credit, so a cash shortfall has no financing backstop (IFE weakness #6, 3.5.2). An unrelated kiosk business creates an independent income stream that doesn't compete with the restaurant for working capital, and captures commuters whose dwell time is too short for a sit-down or made-to-order döner in the first place.

Main risk: it draws scarce owner capital and management attention away from a core store whose IFE score (2.51) is only just above average (3.9), toward an operating skillset — equipment, vending logistics — the founding team does not currently have.

Book guidelines: no strong guideline is met (no spare capital or managerial talent; markets not saturated). Not recommended.

4.6.3 Defensive Strategies

Defensive strategies protect the business when sales, cash flow or market position are under pressure, instead of pursuing further growth. David groups three here — retrenchment, divestiture and liquidation. Döner Street has only one store and one business line, so there is nothing to divest; only retrenchment and liquidation apply.

1. Retrenchment Strategy: Cost Reduction and Operational Efficiency

Objective: to reduce operating costs, minimize financial risks, and improve profitability while maintaining the quality of Döner Street's products and services.

Actions:

  • Control food waste: carefully manage ingredient quantities and inventory to avoid unnecessary losses.
  • Negotiate with suppliers: find more affordable suppliers and negotiate better prices for meat, vegetables, bread and sauces.
  • Focus on profitable products: prioritize the döner sizes and combos that generate the best profit margins.
  • Optimize staffing and schedules: assign employees according to customer demand to avoid unnecessary labor costs.
  • Reduce unnecessary expenses: control electricity, water, packaging and other operating costs without compromising food safety or quality.

Expected results:

  • Lower operating costs.
  • Less food waste.
  • Improved profit margins.
  • Greater financial stability.
  • Continued competitiveness through affordable prices.

Explanation: if necessary, Döner Street will implement this retrenchment strategy to lower operating expenses and improve financial performance, beginning with controlling food waste, negotiating better prices with suppliers, optimizing staff schedules, and focusing on the most profitable products, while also monitoring expenses such as electricity, water and packaging. These measures will help the business improve its profit margins, maintain affordable prices, and remain competitive in Bogotá — all without compromising product quality or customer satisfaction.

Book guidelines: used when a firm fails to meet its objectives over time. Contingency plan, triggered if sales stay below the pessimistic scenario for 3 months in a row.

2. Liquidation

Strategy: if retrenchment does not restore the minimum cash flow needed to operate, and the owners decide not to inject further equity, close the business in an orderly way, selling all assets for their tangible value rather than continuing to operate at a loss.

Actions:

  • Sell production and service equipment (vertical rotisserie, POS terminals, refrigeration) to another food-service operator or through second-hand equipment dealers.
  • Sell the owned premises or rent them out, and settle pending obligations to suppliers, staff and taxes in priority order before distributing any remaining cash to owners.
  • Close the delivery-app listings and the Google Business Profile, settling outstanding stamp-card rewards (4.4.1) before the final closing date.

Objective: recover the maximum possible value from the business's physical assets and close all legal, tax and payroll obligations cleanly, protecting the owners' remaining equity instead of running a 100%-equity-funded business (3.5.2) at a continuing loss.

Book guideline: liquidation is used only when retrenchment has failed and the owners can reduce their losses by selling the assets in parts (equipment net value ≈ COP 51M at the end of 2027).

How we differentiate inside the niche (focused differentiation)

Strategy statement. A döner-anchored combo — protein, side and drink sold together, not a single wrap — made to order with a clear German identity, served fast and backed by a written service guarantee.

Döner Street is not the only operator selling a döner-style wrap: Shawarma Petra sells a comparable product (2.5.2), and the sliced, spit-roasted format is close enough to shawarma and gyros that a first-time customer could treat all three as substitutes. So the edge cannot rest on being the only seller of the category. It rests on three things a rival would have to copy all at once: specializing the whole menu around one format instead of diluting it (Filiák's broad menu, 2.5.3), selling it as a fixed-price combo with a side and drink rather than a standalone item, and backing it with the systems and guarantees below.

What makes us different

  • Identity and specialization: the menu has six products and no other protein concept, so every peso of marketing and every minute of staff training reinforces one format instead of spreading across a broad menu like Filiák's.
  • Product: meat carved from a vertical spit, pita warmed to order and fries fried fresh (about 4 minutes) — not an assembly of pre-cooked parts.
  • Format: every order is a combo — döner, fries and a drink — priced and sold as one unit (3.4.2), not a döner alone, which is why the comparison that matters is combo-to-combo against rivals, not wrap-to-wrap.
  • Service: a Fast Service Guarantee of 10 minutes, with compensation if an order takes more than 15 minutes, plus five more customer-experience warranties (3.4.3). A customer's patience threshold is 10 to 15 minutes, after which students switch to an instantly available substitute (2.6.3).
  • System: a point-of-sale record of every order's timing, digital cold-chain and HACCP logs, and a quality check before the order leaves the counter (3.6.1). None of the three budget-comparable competitors combines cloud POS, digital HACCP and an owned ordering channel (2.4.6).

Type 5 — Focus, Best Value (Main Focus Strategy)

Strategy statement. The best-value döner combo for students and young people who want a fast, filling alternative to traditional fast food: more food, more freshness and more guarantees for every peso — sold as a combo, not competing on the döner wrap alone.

Döner Street does not win this niche by being the only place selling a döner-style wrap — Shawarma Petra already does, and arepas and other fast-food formats compete for the same budget (2.6.3). It wins by being the best value inside the niche: the combo (protein, side and drink) with the most beef per peso and the clearest guarantees, for a buyer group the incumbents have not locked down.

Target niche

Students and young people in Bogotá. None of the three incumbents has locked in strong loyalty with students (2.6.1): Gyros & Kebab skews to adults and office workers, Shawarma Petra to tourists and executives (even though it is the one direct döner-style rival, 2.6.1), and Filiák to higher-income diners. In our survey, 47% order fast food weekly or more, 63% prefer to eat in the store, 24% order delivery and 13% take away (3.4.1).

The offer and its price position

About 49% of consumers are highly price-sensitive (2.2.2), so the offer has to be clear value, not a premium. Our combos sit inside the price bands of the competitors profiled.

How it works

  1. A döner-only menu with six products and common ingredients, so we stay specialists and keep waste low.
  2. Value promotions, not price cuts: 2x1 (preferred by 40% of respondents as a first-purchase incentive), a combo with a free drink (39%), Refer & Earn and corporate lunches. Every promotion is sized to stay above production cost at our 35% target margin.
  3. Stamp card: five stamps earn a free upgrade to the Large Combo. A reward costs us about COP 3,100 in ingredients, around 2% of what the customer spends in those five visits, and we keep the program only if at least 30% of redeemers order a Large Combo again within three months (4.4.1).
  4. Upgrade prompt at the register: every Medium Combo order is offered the Large Combo as the best-value option.
  5. Guarantees as part of the value: the speed promise and the other warranties (3.4.3) make the price feel safe.
