3. Perform Internal Audit

David's Strategic Management frames the internal audit around the basic business functions — management, marketing, finance/accounting, production/operations, R&D, and management information systems — to surface the firm's own strengths and weaknesses. This page covers the Management and Marketing functions, starting with how Döner Street is organized and continuing with how it plans to identify, reach, and keep its customers.

3.3 Management

David identifies five basic management functions — planning, organizing, motivating, staffing, and controlling — that together make up the first internal-audit area a firm should examine, because how well a company performs these functions determines whether every other function (marketing, finance, operations) can actually be executed. Döner Street is a single-store, founder-led operation, so the function with the most immediate strategic weight at this stage is organizing: deciding who does what, who reports to whom, and how the roles that run the store day to day fit together.

3.3.1 Planning

David describes planning as the process of setting objectives and deciding in advance how to reach them, and he singles out objectives as the bridge between the firm's mission and the day-to-day work of every position. For Döner Street, planning at this stage means translating the store's goals into concrete, SMART objectives — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound — assigned to each role, so that everyone knows what they are accountable for delivering. The cards below set out three objectives per position.

Store Manager

  • Specific: Focuses on increasing sales.

  • Relevant: Sales growth is essential for the Doner Kebab business.
  • Time-bound: Within the next year.

Objective: Achieve a customer satisfaction rate of at least 90% within the next year.

  • Specific: Focuses on customer satisfaction and service.
  • Measurable: 90% satisfaction rate.
  • Achievable: Attainable through ongoing staff training and steady operational improvements over the year.
  • Relevant: Customer satisfaction encourages loyalty and repeat purchases.
  • Time-bound: Within the next year.

  • Specific: Focuses on reducing food waste.
  • Measurable: 15% reduction.
  • Achievable: Attainable with sustained inventory and production-management improvements throughout the year.
  • Relevant: Reduces costs and improves profitability.

3.3.2 Organizing

David defines organizing as encompassing organizational structure, job design and specialization, span of control, chain of command, and coordination among positions and departments — in short, the design work that turns strategy into a set of roles people can actually be hired for. For Döner Street, the Founding Team retains strategic and financial ownership, but the Store Manager is the head of the store structure and holds full day-to-day operational authority. The Store Manager directly supervises a team of six Store Associates through a continuous chain of command, and coordinates two functional support areas — Accounting and Sales and Social Media — through dotted, staff-type relationships rather than direct line authority. This flat, single-layer structure keeps the span of control wide but manageable for a one-store operation and concentrates accountability in a single operational head.

3.3.3 Motivating

David frames motivating as inspiring people to perform at their best, and stresses that non-financial levers — job design, participation, recognition, and a good working environment — sustain engagement at least as well as pay. Döner Street builds its motivation strategy around its two internal payroll roles — six Store Associates and one Store Manager — giving each its own set of non-monetary incentives suited to its place in the structure.

Store Associate

  • Shift planning published at least two weeks in advance, with employees able to request preferred shifts
  • Employee of the Month, chosen by the team, gets first pick of shifts for the following week
  • Free food and drinks during every shift
  • The team on shift chooses the in-store music during off-peak hours

Store Manager

  • Full autonomy over day-to-day operating decisions, without needing sign-off from the Founding Team for routine matters
  • Public recognition from the Founding Team — a “Store of the Month” shout-out — whenever store KPIs are met
  • First priority on personal shift and vacation scheduling, set before the rest of the team's rotation is published
  • Final say in hiring decisions for new Store Associates, from shortlisting through the final interview

3.3.5 Controlling

David defines controlling as verifying whether actual results match the objectives set in planning, using measurable indicators that let each role track its own progress and correct course when needed. The KPIs below track the Store Manager's sales-growth objective and the Store Associate's operational-continuity objective, both defined in section 3.3.1.

Store Manager — Increase Sales Performance

Objective

Increase the store's monthly sales by 10% within the next year by promoting combo meals and improving customer service at our Döner Kebab restaurant.

KPI: Monthly Sales Growth Rate

This KPI measures the percentage increase or decrease in the store's sales compared with the previous month or the same month last year.

Sales Growth Rate = Current Sales − Previous Sales Previous Sales × 100
Example: if the Döner Kebab store sold $20,000,000 COP last month and $22,000,000 COP this month, the sales growth rate is 10% — meaning the store achieved its objective for that month.
Why this KPI matters

It helps the Store Manager evaluate whether sales strategies, combo promotions, and customer-service improvements are generating more revenue. The manager reviews this KPI every month to track progress toward the one-year goal.

Store Associate — Improve Operational Continuity

Objective

Maintain a 95% on-time order fulfillment rate within the next year, ensuring consistent service even during unexpected customer surges or equipment slowdowns.

KPI: On-Time Order Fulfillment Rate

This KPI measures the percentage of orders fulfilled within the standard time window out of the total number of orders placed.

On-Time Rate = Orders Fulfilled Within Standard Time Total Number of Orders × 100
Target: ≥ 95% per month, sustained across all 12 months.
Ownership

Store Associates are responsible for maintaining pace at their assigned station (cashier or assembly) and flagging bottlenecks in real time (e.g., missing ingredients, equipment slowdowns, unexpected volume) so issues can be addressed before they affect fulfillment time.

Why this KPI matters

This KPI directly reflects the customer's experience at the moment of truth: how quickly and consistently their order gets fulfilled. It helps Store Associates understand fulfillment as something customers notice immediately, and how delays drive dissatisfaction, complaints, and lost repeat business. It also gives Store Associates a clear, shared standard to work toward, making it easy to see whether day-to-day execution is actually translating into reliable service — especially during unexpected surges when operational continuity matters most.

3.4 Marketing

David identifies seven basic marketing functions — customer analysis, selling products/services, product and service planning, pricing, distribution, marketing research, and cost/benefit analysis — that together define how a firm identifies, reaches, and keeps its customers. For Döner Street, pricing decisions are addressed together with Selling Products/Services (3.4.2) rather than as a standalone subsection, and marketing research is carried out through the customer survey in Customer Analysis (3.4.1) rather than as its own subsection, so the five areas below cover customer analysis, selling, product and service planning, distribution, and cost/benefit analysis in turn.

3.4.1 Customer Analysis

David describes customer analysis as examining and evaluating consumer needs, wants, and characteristics through customer profiles and market surveys — the starting point for every other marketing decision. For a single store opening next to Universidad de La Sabana, that means answering one question first: who is actually going to walk through the door, and what do they need from a döner kebab that the three closest competitors (Gyros & Kebab, Shawarma Petra, and Filiák Cocina Griega, covered in 2.5–2.8) aren't already giving them.

Customer Profile

Data-Driven Profile — The Occasional, Flavor-First Explorer

  • Consumption habit: frequent eaters have now edged past occasional ones — 47% order weekly or more (31% once a week, 12% several times a week, 4% daily) versus 44% who order once or twice a month. 52% order by walking directly into a restaurant, with Rappi/DiDi Food (23%) and the restaurant's own app/WhatsApp (22%) close behind.
  • What drives the choice: flavor is still the clear top factor (64%), with ingredient quality close behind (56%) and portion size third (46%) — taste and quality, not price alone (43%), are the primary levers.
  • Awareness of Döner Kebab: 45% still didn't know the category existed, 31% have heard of it but not tried it, and 25% already know and have tried it. 84% don't currently visit any of the three named oriental/Greek competitors (Gyros & Kebab, Shawarma Petra, Filiák), so Döner House is mostly introducing the category rather than pulling loyal customers away from a rival.
  • Willingness to try: despite that awareness gap, 81% say they're "dispuesto(a)" or "muy dispuesto(a)" to try it at a new venture, and only a single respondent (1%) said they were reluctant — low awareness looks like an education opportunity, not a demand problem.
  • Product preference: beef (47%) is still the preferred protein, with lamb close behind (25%) and chicken at 19%; a medium-sized döner (75%) is the dominant order size, and fries (57%) have regained the lead over cheese (53%) and house sauce (51%) as the most-wanted add-on.
  • Budget: 61% expect to pay $15,000–$25,000 COP for a combo (fries + drink); the top bracket has shifted up slightly — $20,001–$25,000 (33%), $25,001–$30,000 (32%), and $15,000–$20,000 (29%) — still no single price point dominates.
  • Occasion: a clear preference for eating in-store (63%) over delivery (24%), with take-away third (13%).
  • Speed: 90% rate sub-15-minute service at least moderately important, and "extremely important" has grown to 18%, though "moderately important" (37%) is still the single leading answer — flavor and quality still lead the decision.
  • What would trigger a first purchase: 2x1 offers have edged ahead (40%), just past a free drink-and-side combo (39%); straight discounts (17%) and loyalty points (5%) trail well behind.
  • Naming signal: "Döner Street" has widened its lead over "Döner Haus" (39% vs. 30%); respondents most want a name that's short/punchy (50%) or easy to remember (48%), ahead of product-identifying framing (41%).

  • Secondary goal: a quick, low-friction visit, mostly eating in (63%) rather than ordering delivery (24%).
  • What moves them to act: a concrete first-purchase incentive (a 2x1 or a free side) more than a loyalty program, which only 5% picked.

3.4.3 Product and Service Planning

David describes product and service planning as covering decisions such as test marketing, product positioning, branding, packaging, and warranties — the choices that shape how customers experience what the firm sells day to day. For Döner Street, that warranty decision is formalized below as six Customer Experience Warranties, each with its own trigger, resolution, approval level and internal record, so every Store Associate and supervisor knows exactly what to do the moment a customer raises a problem.

Customer Experience Warranties

These warranties focus on the customer’s experience when purchasing and consuming our Döner Kebab. They complement the product warranties by ensuring that customers receive reliable, professional, and satisfactory service.

1. Fast Service Guarantee

Warranty: we guarantee that customers will receive their order within 10 minutes of placing it. If an unexpected delay occurs, the customer will be informed.

When to claim: the customer may claim this warranty when the order takes longer than 15 minutes without prior notification.

Resolution: the employee in charge will first explain the reason for the delay and provide an estimated waiting time. If the delay is considered excessive, the store manager may offer a reasonable service recovery, such as a discount, complimentary beverage, or another benefit according to company policy.

Who can approve it: the store manager can approve compensation.

Claim process:

  1. Customer reports the delay to an employee.
  2. Employee verifies the order and preparation time.
  3. Employee informs the store manager if compensation is required.
  4. Store manager determines the appropriate solution.
  5. Customer receives the solution and the case is recorded.

Internal record: the employee records the date, order number, reason for the delay, customer complaint, solution provided, and employee/store manager responsible.

2. Order Accuracy Guarantee

Warranty: we guarantee that customers will receive the order they requested, including the selected ingredients, sauces, size, and other preferences.

When to claim: the customer may claim this warranty when an item, ingredient, sauce, or customization is missing or different from what was requested.

Resolution: the organization will correct the order at no additional cost, within 5 minutes of the report while the customer waits. If correcting the order is not possible within that window or causes excessive additional waiting time, the supervisor may authorize a replacement or another appropriate compensation.

Who can approve it: the shift supervisor or store manager.

Claim process:

  1. Customer reports the incorrect order.
  2. Employee checks the original order.
  3. The mistake is verified.
  4. The kitchen prepares the correction.
  5. The corrected order is delivered to the customer.
  6. The incident is recorded internally.

Internal record: the organization records the order number, incorrect item, requested item, cause of the error, corrective action, and person responsible.

3. Friendly Customer Service Guarantee

Warranty: we guarantee that every employee will greet the customer, confirm the order back to them, and address requests without raising their voice, interrupting, or using offensive or discriminatory language.

When to claim: the customer may claim this warranty if an employee raises their voice, interrupts them, uses offensive or discriminatory language, or fails to greet the customer or confirm the order.

Resolution: the store manager will listen to the customer within 10 minutes if still on-site, or within 24 hours if the complaint is reported afterward (phone, WhatsApp, or social media). The manager will apologize when appropriate, investigate the situation, and take the necessary corrective action. Depending on the case, the employee may receive additional customer-service training or a formal internal warning.

Who can approve it: the store manager.

Claim process:

  1. Customer reports the situation.
  2. Store manager listens to the complaint.
  3. The employee involved is asked for their version of the situation.
  4. The store manager determines the appropriate corrective action.
  5. The customer is informed that the situation has been addressed.
  6. The case is documented.

Internal record: a customer-service incident report should include the date, description of the situation, employees involved, customer complaint, investigation, corrective action, and store manager responsible.

4. Clean and Comfortable Environment Guarantee

Warranty: we guarantee that our service and dining areas will be maintained clean, organized, and suitable for customers.

When to claim: the customer may claim this warranty when the dining or service area does not meet the organization’s established cleanliness standards.

Resolution: the responsible employee will address the problem immediately, within 5 minutes of the report. If necessary, the supervisor will assign additional cleaning or maintenance activities.

Who can approve it: the shift supervisor or store manager.

Claim process:

  1. Customer reports the problem.
  2. Employee or supervisor verifies the condition.
  3. Cleaning or maintenance is performed immediately.
  4. Supervisor verifies that the problem has been resolved.
  5. The incident is recorded if necessary.

Internal record: the organization maintains cleaning checklists and incident records showing the date, area affected, problem identified, corrective action, and employee responsible.

5. Customer Satisfaction Guarantee

Warranty: we are committed to providing a positive customer experience. If a customer is dissatisfied with the service, we will listen to their concern and provide an appropriate solution.

When to claim: the customer may claim this warranty when their overall service experience does not meet the organization’s established standards.

Resolution: the supervisor will evaluate the situation within 10 minutes if the customer is still in the store, or within 24 hours if reported afterward, and may offer a replacement, discount, complimentary item, or another reasonable solution depending on the circumstances.

Who can approve it: the shift supervisor can approve minor compensations. The store manager must approve higher-value compensation or exceptional cases.

Claim process:

  1. Customer communicates the dissatisfaction.
  2. Employee listens and collects the relevant information.
  3. Supervisor evaluates the case.
  4. An appropriate solution is offered.
  5. Customer confirms whether the solution addresses the concern.
  6. The case is recorded for future analysis.

Internal record: the organization records the customer complaint, order number, reason for dissatisfaction, solution provided, value of any compensation, and responsible supervisor.

6. Transparent Pricing Guarantee

Warranty: we guarantee that customers will receive clear information about prices, promotions, additional charges, and available options before completing their purchase.

When to claim: the customer may claim this warranty if they are charged an amount that differs from the clearly communicated price without an appropriate explanation.

Resolution: the organization will verify the price within 5 minutes at the register and, if an error occurred, correct the charge or refund the difference when applicable.

Who can approve it: the cashier or store manager can resolve standard pricing errors. The store manager handles exceptional cases.

Claim process:

  1. Customer reports the price difference.
  2. Employee verifies the menu, promotion, or sales system.
  3. The correct price is determined.
  4. Any necessary adjustment or refund is made.
  5. The incident is recorded.

Internal record: the record should include the order number, original charge, correct price, difference, reason for the error, adjustment made, and employee responsible.

General Warranty Policy

All experience warranties are intended to provide a fair and consistent way to resolve customer concerns. Compensation is not automatic in every case; it depends on the circumstances and the severity of the issue.

Approval Levels

  • Employee: listen to the customer, verify basic information, and correct simple service issues that do not involve compensation.
  • Shift Supervisor: approve standard service recovery measures such as a replacement, complimentary beverage, small discount, or similar compensation.
  • Store Manager: approve higher-value compensation, refunds outside standard procedures, serious complaints, or repeated service problems.
  • Management: review recurring problems and decide whether changes to procedures, training, or policies are necessary.

  2. Employee listens and collects the necessary information.
  3. The order or situation is verified.
  4. The supervisor is involved when approval is required.
  5. An appropriate resolution is provided.
  6. The customer is informed of the solution.
  7. The case is recorded internally.
  8. Recurring issues are reviewed by management to prevent them from happening again.

  • Order number

  • Verification performed
  • Resolution provided

3.5 Finance/Accounting

David groups finance/accounting decisions into three areas (Van Horne): the investment decision (what capital to commit and to what), the financing decision (how to fund it), and the dividend decision (how much profit to distribute versus reinvest). For Döner Street: the investment decision is the equipment and fit-out CapEx below (no property purchase — the premises are owned outright, so there is no rent line); the financing decision is 100% owner equity with no debt; and the dividend decision, for the first year, is to retain 100% of any profit rather than distribute it, since a first-year store needs the cash cushion more than the owners need a payout. Every monetary figure in this section is in Colombian pesos (COP).

3.5.2 Finance Resources — Three 2027 Scenarios

Every cost already committed elsewhere on this page — payroll (3.3.2), the recomputed marketing budget (3.4.5), and the MIS subscriptions (3.8) — is consolidated here into a full three-statement model (income statement, balance sheet, cash flow), built three times under three different demand assumptions, so the plan shows what a realistic year looks like, what a strong year looks like, and what a bad year would actually do to the business, instead of three cosmetic variations on the same number.

Correction from the prior version

Rebuilding this section against a full accounting structure surfaced a double-count that was in the previous P&L: 3.4.2’s per-unit standard cost (Direct Labor $1,750/combo, used only to set the $28,400 menu price via cost-plus) was being charged inside Cost of Goods Sold, and the 6 Store Associates’ real fixed payroll ($187,200,000/yr) was also being charged in full via the old bundled Operating Expenses line — the same people’s wages, counted twice. This model charges their real fixed payroll once, as Direct Labor inside Cost of Goods Sold below (since Store Associates are the production labor), and leaves the $1,750/combo figure in 3.4.2 doing only what it was meant to do: setting the menu price. Net profit is higher across all three scenarios as a result of that fix. Two more cost lines were added after this correction — delivery aggregator commission and delivery/ take-away packaging (see Assumptions below) — which bring the realistic scenario back down to a modest $37,622,940 net profit (2.9% margin) and push the pessimistic scenario into a real loss again: −$135,349,412 (−18.2% margin), negative owner’s equity of −$5,349,412, and a negative cash balance of −$37,229,815 at year-end. Fixed labor cost still doesn’t shrink when volume drops; that risk was never the double-count, and it survives both this fix and the later cost additions.

No debt, no trade credit, no bank interest

Döner Street is a single, 100%-equity-funded store, not a company large enough to run on supplier credit or bank financing — so this model does not carry a Cuentas por Cobrar, Proveedores (trade payable), Otras Cuentas por Pagar, bank loan, or overdraft line anywhere in the three statements. Suppliers and service providers are paid in cash as costs are incurred, and no customer is extended credit. There is also no Ingresos Financieros (interest income) line: cash sits on hand rather than in an interest-bearing account. The only liabilities modeled are payroll, social-security, and tax accruals — obligations required by Colombian law regardless of financing structure, not discretionary debt. One consequence: the Pessimistic scenario’s cash shortfall is no longer absorbed by a modeled bank overdraft — it shows up directly as a negative Efectivo balance, which is the honest signal that fresh owner capital would be required, not a financing product this business actually has access to.

3.7 Research and Development

David draws a basic distinction here: internal R&D, run by a firm’s own department, versus contract R&D, bought from independent researchers or agencies — and notes that many small firms have no formal R&D department at all. Döner Street is exactly that kind of firm: a single store with no dedicated R&D role, so the honest answer is that product development is informal and internal, run by an existing role rather than a new department.

3.7.1 R&D Ownership and Process

Who

The Store Manager owns product development, since they already hold full day-to-day operational authority (3.3.2). They draw on three inputs that already exist elsewhere on this page rather than a separate research function:

  • The customer survey (3.4.1) — what customers say they want.
  • The Sales & Social Media agency’s engagement data (3.3.2) — an external, contract-R&D input on which content and offers actually perform.
  • The shift-log customer-feedback loop (3.4.3) — day-to-day signal on what is and isn’t working with the current menu.

How

A lightweight stage-gate, reusing tools this page already defines instead of building a parallel process:

  1. An idea surfaces from the survey, the agency’s data, or shift-log feedback.
  2. The Store Manager costs the recipe using the same method as every current menu item (3.4.2): raw materials + labor + CIF, checked against the 35% target margin.
  3. If it clears the margin, it runs as a limited-time trial (e.g. a weekend special).
  4. Sales and feedback are measured for 2–4 weeks.
  5. Go/no-go decision: add it to the permanent menu, or drop it.

Budget approach: of David’s four common methods (fund every proposal, a percentage of sales, matching competitor spend, or working backward from how many new products are needed), a small store fits the percentage-of-sales approach best — an informal earmark of about 0.5% of revenue for ingredient trial batches, absorbed inside the existing cost of goods rather than as a separate budget line in 3.5.2.
