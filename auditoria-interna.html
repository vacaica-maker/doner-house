3. Perform Internal Audit

David's Strategic Management frames the internal audit around the basic business functions — management, marketing, finance/accounting, production/operations, R&D, and management information systems — to surface the firm's own strengths and weaknesses. This page covers the Management and Marketing functions, starting with how Döner House is organized and continuing with how it plans to identify, reach, and keep its customers.

← Back to Index

3.3 Management

David identifies five basic management functions — planning, organizing, motivating, staffing, and controlling — that together make up the first internal-audit area a firm should examine, because how well a company performs these functions determines whether every other function (marketing, finance, operations) can actually be executed. Döner House is a single-store, founder-led operation, so the function with the most immediate strategic weight at this stage is organizing: deciding who does what, who reports to whom, and how the roles that run the store day to day fit together.

3.3.1 Planning

David describes planning as the process of setting objectives and deciding in advance how to reach them, and he singles out objectives as the bridge between the firm's mission and the day-to-day work of every position. For Döner House, planning at this stage means translating the store's goals into concrete, SMART objectives — specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound — assigned to each role, so that everyone knows what they are accountable for delivering. The cards below set out three objectives per position.

Store Manager

1. Increase Sales Performance

Objective: Increase the store’s monthly sales by 10% within the next year.

  • Specific: Focuses on increasing sales.
  • Measurable: 10% increase.
  • Achievable: Realistic over a full year through consistent promotions and improved sales techniques.
  • Relevant: Sales growth is essential for the Doner Kebab business.
  • Time-bound: Within the next year.
2. Improve Customer Satisfaction

Objective: Achieve a customer satisfaction rate of at least 90% within the next year.

  • Specific: Focuses on customer satisfaction and service.
  • Measurable: 90% satisfaction rate.
  • Achievable: Attainable through ongoing staff training and steady operational improvements over the year.
  • Relevant: Customer satisfaction encourages loyalty and repeat purchases.
  • Time-bound: Within the next year.
3. Reduce Food Waste

Objective: Reduce food waste by 15% within the next year.

  • Specific: Focuses on reducing food waste.
  • Measurable: 15% reduction.
  • Achievable: Attainable with sustained inventory and production-management improvements throughout the year.
  • Relevant: Reduces costs and improves profitability.
  • Time-bound: Within the next year.

Store Associate

1. Strengthen Workforce Flexibility

Objective: Achieve 100% cross-training coverage among Store Associates within the next year in case of team member absence or turnover.

  • Specific: Targets full cross-training coverage as a safeguard against staffing gaps.
  • Measurable: 100% of Store Associates trained in both cashier and assembly roles.
  • Achievable: Realistic given the store's staff size and shift rotation.
  • Relevant: Ensures continuous station coverage regardless of unexpected absences or turnover.
  • Time-bound: To be achieved within the next year.

  • Specific: Targets the reduction of stockout incidents during demand surges.
  • Measurable: 90% reduction compared to current stockout frequency.

  • Time-bound: To be achieved within the next year.

3.3.2 Organizing

David defines organizing as encompassing organizational structure, job design and specialization, span of control, chain of command, and coordination among positions and departments — in short, the design work that turns strategy into a set of roles people can actually be hired for. For Döner House, the Founding Team retains strategic and financial ownership, but the Store Manager is the head of the store structure and holds full day-to-day operational authority. The Store Manager directly supervises a team of six Store Associates through a continuous chain of command, and coordinates two functional support areas — Accounting and Sales and Social Media — through dotted, staff-type relationships rather than direct line authority. This flat, single-layer structure keeps the span of control wide but manageable for a one-store operation and concentrates accountability in a single operational head.

3.3.3 Motivating

David frames motivating as inspiring people to perform at their best, and stresses that non-financial levers — job design, participation, recognition, and a good working environment — sustain engagement at least as well as pay. With a six-person store team, Döner House builds its motivation strategy around the two internal payroll roles, giving each one its own set of non-monetary incentives suited to its place in the structure.

Store Associate

  • Shift planning published at least two weeks in advance, with employees able to request preferred shifts
  • Employee of the Month, chosen by the team, gets first pick of shifts for the following week
  • Free food and drinks during every shift
  • The team on shift chooses the in-store music during off-peak hours

Store Manager

  • Full autonomy over day-to-day operating decisions, without needing sign-off from the Founding Team for routine matters
  • Public recognition from the Founding Team — a “Store of the Month” shout-out — whenever store KPIs are met
  • First priority on personal shift and vacation scheduling, set before the rest of the team's rotation is published
  • Final say in hiring decisions for new Store Associates, from shortlisting through the final interview

3.3.5 Controlling

David defines controlling as verifying whether actual results match the objectives set in planning, using measurable indicators that let each role track its own progress and correct course when needed. The KPIs below track the Store Manager's sales-growth objective and the Store Associate's operational-continuity objective, both defined in section 3.3.1.

Store Manager — Increase Sales Performance

Objective

Increase the store's monthly sales by 10% within the next year by promoting combo meals and improving customer service at our Döner Kebab restaurant.

KPI: Monthly Sales Growth Rate

This KPI measures the percentage increase or decrease in the store's sales compared with the previous month or the same month last year.

Sales Growth Rate = Current Sales − Previous Sales Previous Sales × 100
Example: if the Döner Kebab store sold $20,000,000 COP last month and $22,000,000 COP this month, the sales growth rate is 10% — meaning the store achieved its objective for that month.
Why this KPI matters

It helps the Store Manager evaluate whether sales strategies, combo promotions, and customer-service improvements are generating more revenue. The manager reviews this KPI every month to track progress toward the one-year goal.

Store Associate — Improve Operational Continuity

Objective

Maintain a 95% on-time order fulfillment rate within the next year, ensuring consistent service even during unexpected customer surges or equipment slowdowns.

KPI: On-Time Order Fulfillment Rate

This KPI measures the percentage of orders fulfilled within the standard time window out of the total number of orders placed.

On-Time Rate = Orders Fulfilled Within Standard Time Total Number of Orders × 100
Target: ≥ 95% per month, sustained across all 12 months.
Ownership

Store Associates are responsible for maintaining pace at their assigned station (cashier or assembly) and flagging bottlenecks in real time (e.g., missing ingredients, equipment slowdowns, unexpected volume) so issues can be addressed before they affect fulfillment time.

Why this KPI matters

This KPI directly reflects the customer's experience at the moment of truth: how quickly and consistently their order gets fulfilled. It helps Store Associates understand fulfillment as something customers notice immediately, and how delays drive dissatisfaction, complaints, and lost repeat business. It also gives Store Associates a clear, shared standard to work toward, making it easy to see whether day-to-day execution is actually translating into reliable service — especially during unexpected surges when operational continuity matters most.

3.4 Marketing

David identifies seven basic marketing functions — customer analysis, selling products/services, product and service planning, pricing, distribution, marketing research, and cost/benefit analysis — that together define how a firm identifies, reaches, and keeps its customers. For Döner House, pricing decisions are addressed together with Selling Products/Services (3.4.2) rather than as a standalone subsection, so the six areas below cover customer analysis, selling, product and service planning, distribution, marketing research, and opportunity analysis in turn.

3.4.1 Customer Analysis

David describes customer analysis as examining and evaluating consumer needs, wants, and characteristics through customer profiles and market surveys — the starting point for every other marketing decision. For a single store opening next to Universidad de La Sabana, that means answering one question first: who is actually going to walk through the door, and what do they need from a döner kebab that the three closest competitors (Gyros & Kebab, Shawarma Petra, and Filiák Cocina Griega, covered in 2.5–2.8) aren't already giving them.

Customer Profile (pending validation)

The profile below is a preliminary hypothesis built from secondary data (store location and the competitive analysis in 2.5–2.8), not yet from customer responses. It will be rebuilt using the results of the survey below once enough responses come in.

Primary — The La Sabana Student

  • Who: undergraduate and graduate students at Universidad de La Sabana, roughly 18–25 years old, moving along the Autopista Norte / Calle 72 corridor between campus and home.
  • Need: a fast, filling meal between classes — ideally under 10–15 minutes, the documented patience threshold before a hungry student switches to a cheaper, instantly available substitute like arepas.
  • Budget: price-sensitive; weighs Döner Kebab against $10,000–$18,000 substitutes, so combo value and portion size matter as much as ingredient quality.
  • Occasion: weekday lunch or dinner between classes, solo or with one or two classmates; dine-in or takeaway.
  • Why this segment is the priority: none of the three closest competitors has locked in loyalty here — Gyros & Kebab skews to office workers and adult consumers, Shawarma Petra to tourists, executives, and nightlife, and Filiák to high-purchasing-power professionals — leaving the student segment open for Döner House to win.

Secondary — The Corridor Commuter

  • Who: residents and office workers along the Autopista Norte / Calle 72 corridor, including the growing share of single-person households (20.2% of Colombian households by 2025) who need fast, individual meals.
  • Need: convenience on the way home — quick pickup or delivery, reliable quality without dining in.
  • Occasion: evening meal, usually solo, delivery or takeaway.

Döner Kebab Assembly Time

Maximum production capacity starts with how long it actually takes to build one döner kebab at the assembly station: carving meat from the vertical spit, warming the pita bread, adding toppings, and wrapping the order — plus, for Combos, portioning the already-fried fries and pouring the drink. Carving and topping time scale slightly with size; toasting the bread and wrapping do not.

Station stepSmallMediumLarge
Carve meat from the spit15 sec20 sec25 sec
Warm the pita bread40 sec40 sec40 sec
Add toppings & sauce30 sec35 sec40 sec
Wrap & pack15 sec15 sec15 sec
Total — Without Combo100 sec (~1.7 min)110 sec (~1.8 min)120 sec (2.0 min)
+ Portion fries & pour drink (Combo only)15 sec15 sec15 sec
Total — Combo115 sec (~1.9 min)125 sec (~2.1 min)135 sec (2.25 min)

Frying is done ahead in batches on the fryer running in parallel, so it doesn't add to the per-order assembly line — only plating the already-fried portion does. Averaged across all six product/modality combinations, assembly takes ~117.5 seconds (about 2 minutes) per order, the figure used to size capacity below.

3.4.3 Product and Service Planning

David describes product and service planning as covering decisions such as test marketing, product positioning, branding, packaging, and warranties — the choices that shape how customers experience what the firm sells day to day. For Döner House, the most immediate lever here is a customer satisfaction guarantee: a simple, no-hassle policy that lets any Store Associate fix an order problem on the spot, without manager sign-off or paperwork, since a fast fix at the counter protects the customer relationship far more than a formal claims process would.

Customer Satisfaction Guarantee

  • Coverage: wrong item, incomplete order, cold or poorly prepared food, or an incorrect customization.
  • Resolution: the customer chooses between a free remake on the spot or a full refund — no exceptions, no fine print.
  • Who can approve it: any Store Associate on shift; no manager sign-off or supervisor call needed.
  • When to claim it: before leaving the counter for dine-in orders, or immediately upon receiving a takeout or delivery order.
  • Process: tell the associate what went wrong → the associate remakes the order or processes the refund at the register → done, typically within 5 minutes and with no forms or receipts required.
  • Internal record: the associate logs a one-line entry (item, reason) in the daily shift log, so the Store Manager can spot recurring issues without adding any friction for the customer.
