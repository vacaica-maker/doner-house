5. Generate, Evaluate, and Select Strategies

David's three-stage framework (Ch. 6, Figure 6-2) for choosing among strategic alternatives: the Input Stage scores the external and internal environment, the Matching Stage crosses those scores to generate candidate strategies, and the Decision Stage ranks them with a single objective tool. Every number below reuses the EFE, CPM and IFE matrices already built in Chapters 2 and 3 — nothing is re-scored from scratch.

← Back to Index

5.1 Stage 1: The Input Stage

The Input Stage does not generate strategies — it just scores the raw material every later stage reuses: how attractive the external environment is (EFE), how Döner Street stacks up against the three rivals profiled (CPM), and how strong its internal position is (IFE). All three matrices already exist in this site from Chapters 2 and 3; this section links to them instead of duplicating the tables.

5.1.1 External Factor Evaluation (EFE) Matrix

10 opportunities and 10 threats, each weighted by importance (0.0–1.0, summing to 1.00) and rated 1–4 on how well Döner Street currently responds to it. Total weighted score: 2.75, above the 2.50 midpoint — the external environment is moderately favorable, driven mainly by the tax regime for small restaurants and the growing QSR segment.

See the full matrix in 2.7 Industry Analysis: the EFE Matrix.

The same 12 critical success factors scored for Döner Street and all three profiled rivals. Döner Street leads, narrowly: 2.85 vs. Filiák's 2.84, Shawarma Petra's 2.78 and Gyros & Kebab's 1.95 — ahead on price and student price-to-value fit, behind on brand reputation and digital presence, which is expected for a business that has not launched.

See the full matrix in 2.8 The Competitive Profile Matrix.

5.2 Stage 2: The Matching Stage

Matching tools cross the Input Stage scores to generate strategy candidates — none of them picks a winner by itself; the Decision Stage (5.3) does that. David lists five matching tools (Ch. 6). Döner Street uses four of them: the BCG Matrix is not built, for the book-based reason explained in 5.2.3.

  2. Open 30 minutes earlier (11:30 AM) on weekdays, using spare kitchen capacity ahead of the regular opening, to capture more of the growing QSR lunch rush within walking distance, adding 10% more weekday transactions by mid-2027. (S1, O2, O3)
  3. Use 50% of the Régimen SIMPLE tax savings to keep combo prices below Shawarma Petra and fund a 2x1 Tuesday promotion. (S7, S10, O1, O3)

ST Strengths + Threats

  1. Keep the 10-minute guarantee and add an “Express Small Döner” ready in 5 minutes at $17,100, to compete with arepas. (S1, T7)
  2. Sign 6-month fixed-price contracts with 2 local meat suppliers and allow only one 7% menu price increase per year, to protect the margin from inflation. (S7, S9, T3)
  3. Market the WhatsApp ordering-ahead channel and dine-in combos as the lower-cost alternative to delivery-app prices (no 18%–30% commission passed on anywhere), to win budget-conscious young customers who are already cutting delivery spend. (S8, T4)
  4. Offer a stamp card (5 purchases = free upgrade to a Large Combo) with a goal of 50% of transactions enrolled, to keep young customers who are cutting spending. (S7, S4, T2, T4)
  5. Change to compostable kraft packaging by September 2027, three years before the 2030 ban, with a maximum extra cost of $300 per order, and use it in marketing. (S9, T8)

WO Weaknesses + Opportunities

  1. Put a QR code on every receipt that asks for a Google review (free fries on the next visit), to reach 300 reviews with a 4.5 rating by December 2027. (W1, O5, O8)
  2. Create 2 signature house sauces with local suppliers, test them with 100 customers and register the name and recipe. (W2, O7)
  3. Save the other 50% of the Régimen SIMPLE tax savings in an emergency reserve of $20M by December 2027, so the store can survive low months without a bank loan. (W7, W6, O1)
  4. Sell corporate lunch packs through WhatsApp to offices near the store on the Calle 72 / Autopista Norte corridor, with a goal of 25 packs per week in June, July and December. (W7, O2, O5)
  5. Hire a freelance creator for 2 posts per week (Tuesday and Friday) showing the vertical rotisserie, to reach 3,000 followers in 12 months and stand out from Shawarma Petra. (W1, W9, O3)

WT Weaknesses + Threats

  1. Certify one senior Associate per shift to handle complaints and approve compensation when the Store Manager is absent. (W5, W8, T2)
  2. In January, June, July and December, close at 10 PM instead of 1 AM, buy smaller batches and pause paid Meta Ads, to cut the monthly loss by at least 50%. (W7, T3, T4)
  3. Buy a spare-parts kit and a yearly maintenance contract for the rotisserie, paid in cash, so a breakdown never closes the store for more than 1 day. (W6, T9)
  4. Keep every order in person or on the own WhatsApp channel, with zero aggregator listings, until the store has 100 reviews and a proven track record — skipping both the commission cost and the risk of a brand-new listing with no reviews. (W1, W4, T2)
  5. Compare real POS sales with the forecast every month. If sales stay under the pessimistic scenario for 3 months in a row, start the retrenchment plan, and liquidation if it fails. (W3, W6, T1)

How to read it. SO strategies use strengths to take opportunities. WO strategies fix weaknesses by using opportunities. ST strategies use strengths to reduce threats. WT strategies are defensive: they reduce weaknesses and avoid threats.

Consistency. All strategies keep the döner-only Best Value Focus strategy (4.8), the 10-minute service promise, and the rule of no bank debt and no supplier credit (3.5). All money figures are in Colombian pesos (COP).

5.2.2 Strategic Position and Action Evaluation (SPACE) Matrix

The SPACE Matrix plots a directional vector, on a Cartesian plane, from four scores: two internal dimensions (Financial Position, Competitive Position) and two external dimensions (Stability Position, Industry Position). David (Ch. 6) sets out six steps, followed here in order: (1) choose the variables that define FP, CP, SP and IP; (2) rate every variable — FP and IP from +1 (worst) to +7 (best), CP and SP from −1 (best) to −7 (worst), comparing Döner Street to the three profiled rivals on FP and CP, and to other industries on SP and IP; (3) average each dimension; (4)–(5) plot the four averages, add CP + IP for the x-coordinate and FP + SP for the y-coordinate, and plot that intersection point; (6) draw the vector from the origin through the point. None of the variables below are David's own illustrative examples (Table 6-2) — each is a Döner Street-specific fact already established elsewhere in this plan, and the debt/“Leverage” factor used in an earlier draft has been dropped entirely.

Step 6 — draw the vector. Both coordinates are positive, so the vector drawn from the origin through (+0.8, +2.2) sits in the Aggressive quadrant.

An aggressive position (David, Ch. 6) fits a financially strong firm with real competitive advantages in a growing, reasonably stable industry: it can afford market penetration, market development, product development, integration or diversification. The vector is driven mostly by financial strength (FP 5.0) and industry growth (IP 4.2); the weak point is Competitive Position (CP −3.4), which is expected before the brand has any reviews or market share — the same gap the Customer Perspective (4.4.2) is built to close.

This agrees with the Intensive Strategies actually pursued in 4.6.1 (market penetration, product development): the Aggressive quadrant would even afford market development, but that option is screened out on its own terms in 4.6.1 (only 1 of David’s 6 Market Development guidelines is met), not for lack of financial strength. Either way, nothing here calls for a defensive move like retrenchment.

5.2.3 Boston Consulting Group (BCG) Matrix — not built

The BCG Matrix plots a firm's divisions on relative market share (x-axis) against industry growth rate (y-axis) to balance a portfolio of multiple business units or product lines — Stars, Cash Cows, Question Marks and Dogs (David, Ch. 6).

Döner Street is a single store with a single product line (one döner-only menu, no separate divisions or business units to plot against each other), so there is nothing for a BCG Matrix to compare. It would become relevant only if Döner Street later ran multiple, genuinely different business lines (for example, the retail grab-and-go line considered and not pursued in 4.6.2) that needed to be weighed against each other for investment priority.

Product development falls short (2 of 5 met — nothing has launched yet to call “mature,” döner production technology is not fast-changing, and R&D is limited to the Store Manager alone), so it is not pursued as a named strategy either, the same screening market development fails (1 of 6 met) for different reasons. 5.2.5 Grand Strategy Matrix

The Grand Strategy Matrix places a firm by two judgment calls: how fast its market is growing, and how strong its competitive position is (David, Ch. 6). Döner Street competes in a rapidly growing market (QSR +5.8% CAGR, 5.1.1) but, as a brand-new, single-store entrant with zero reviews and zero market share, still has a weak competitive position today, whatever the CPM says about its paper strengths (5.1.2). Quadrant II's menu of options is the widest of the four. Of its six strategies, only market penetration is actually pursued in 4.6.1; market development and product development are both on the same menu but both get screened out there directly on David’s own guidelines (1 of 6 and 2 of 5 met respectively); and the remaining three (horizontal integration, divestiture, liquidation) are rejected for the same reasons already given for Unrelated Diversification (4.6.2) and Liquidation (4.6.3): no spare capital or managerial bench for integration, and no grounds to divest or liquidate a business that has not even opened yet.

How the four Matching Stage tools agree

SWOT (5.2.1), SPACE (5.2.2), the IE Matrix (5.2.4) and the Grand Strategy Matrix (5.2.5) were built independently, from different inputs, and all four put market penetration — the only Intensive Strategy actually pursued in 4.6.1 — on the table. Market development and product development come up in every one of them too, but 4.6.1 is where each gets checked against David’s specific guidelines and screened out (1 of 6 and 2 of 5 met), not pursued in 2027. None of the four points toward integration, diversification or a defensive strategy as the primary move for 2027. The Decision Stage (5.3) now ranks all three Intensive Strategies against each other, Market Development and Product Development included, to show the numbers agree with that screening too.

5.3 Stage 3: The Decision Stage

The Matching Stage narrowed the field to one strategy family without ranking its members. The Decision Stage uses a single quantitative tool — the QSPM — to rank Market Penetration, Market Development and Product Development against each other, using the same weighted factors from the EFE and IFE matrices (5.1.1, 5.1.3). Market Development and Product Development are both kept in this ranking for completeness even though 4.6.1 has already screened them out on David’s own guidelines (1 of 6 and 2 of 5 met); scoring them here shows the numbers agree with that screening independently, rather than taking the guideline check’s word for it.

5.3.1 Quantitative Strategic Planning Matrix (QSPM)

Method (David, Ch. 6): list key external and internal factors with their EFE/IFE weights; for each strategy, assign an Attractiveness Score (AS: 1 = not attractive, 2 = somewhat attractive, 3 = reasonably attractive, 4 = highly attractive) to every factor that actually affects the choice between strategies; multiply weight × AS to get the Total Attractiveness Score (TAS); sum each strategy's column. The highest Sum Total Attractiveness Score (STAS) is the best-supported choice.

To keep the matrix readable, the 16 factors below are the ones that actually differ in relevance across the three strategies — the full 20 EFE and 12 IFE factors are in 5.1.1 and 5.1.3.

Result: Market Penetration wins, 3.59 vs. 2.46 for Market Development and 2.26 for Product Development. It scores best on exactly the factors with the largest weights — the 168-unit/day capacity (0.13), the Calle 72/Metro location (0.10) and the unknown-brand weakness (0.13), which hurts a reputation-dependent channel far more than it hurts selling to customers who can already see the store. This QSPM result is why 4.6.1 treats Market Penetration as the only Intensive Strategy actually pursued in 2027, with Market Development and Product Development both held back — the Decision Stage gives a number to what the Matching Stage tools (5.2) and the guideline checks in 4.6.1 already pointed toward independently. The one piece of Product Development that survives, a small 2-sauce test (4.6.1), is cheap and reversible enough that it does not need to clear the same bar as a full strategy.
