Shift to a Service Economy: GDP

1. Introduction and Structural Transformation

Colombia's economy has undergone a significant structural shift toward tertiarization (the services sector). This process, characterized by a greater share of service activities in the formation of Gross Domestic Product (GDP), reflects a maturing of domestic consumption dynamics and greater integration of value-added chains. Unlike economic models based exclusively on resource extraction, the current approach centers on efficiency, convenience, and responsiveness to the demands of an expanding urban market.

2. The Role of the "Accommodation and Food Services" Sector in GDP

According to DANE (Colombia's National Administrative Department of Statistics), the value added by services has consistently exceeded 58% of national GDP in recent years, making it the largest contributor to the country's wealth. Within this category, the branch of "Wholesale and retail trade; vehicle repair; transportation and storage; accommodation and food services" is fundamental to quarterly growth dynamics. This sector not only contributes significantly to gross value added, but also acts as a barometer of consumer confidence and social mobility in urban centers.

3. Detailed Analysis: The Quick-Service Restaurant (QSR) Industry

The Quick Service Restaurants (QSR) industry has become an autonomous economic engine. Unlike traditional dining, the QSR model is built on standardization, delivery speed, and operational process optimization.

3.2 Market Expansion Trends: Colombia's Fast-Food Market Growth

Colombia's fast-food industry is on a sustained expansion path. According to Expert Market Research, the sector is projected to grow at a 5.8% CAGR through 2034, driven by rapid urbanization and rising demand for convenient dining. This growth is backed by strong daily consumer traffic: fast-food restaurants across Colombia receive an estimated 1.5 million visits per day, and roughly 1 in 4 Colombians aged 5–64 eat fast food weekly — a share expected to keep rising as the millennial population grows.

4. Key Drivers of Expansion

This segment's growth is not accidental; it responds to structural changes in consumer behavior and technology:

  • Digitalization of Demand — The massive adoption of last-mile delivery platforms allows quick-service establishments to scale sales without needing to expand physical dine-in infrastructure.
  • Urban Density — Growing urbanization in Colombia centralizes consumption in high-density nodes, favoring fast-food models due to constant foot traffic and the need for quick meal solutions.
  • Process Optimization — Recipe standardization and inventory management through information systems allow fast-food chains to reduce variable costs and increase contribution margin, positively impacting GDP through spending efficiency.

5. Impact Measurement Methodology

This industry's impact on GDP is measured through companies' gross value added, considering total output minus intermediate consumption. For DANE, quick-service establishments are classified under precise definitions that allow household consumption to be broken down. As a highly labor-intensive industry, the sector also drives direct and indirect job creation, which translates into increased final household consumption — an essential component of GDP structure.

6. Conclusion

The shift toward a service economy in Colombia is a well-established reality. The fast-food sector, being intrinsically linked to urban lifestyle and digitalization, positions itself as a key piece of the GDP puzzle. Analyzing its growth — based on operational efficiency and the ability to adapt to new consumption technologies — helps us understand the vitality of the services sector in today's economy.

Opportunities

  • A services sector at 58% of GDP and a QSR segment growing at 5.8% CAGR (1.5M daily visits, 1 in 4 Colombians eating fast food weekly) represent a large, expanding addressable market for a new entrant.

Threats

  • The same growth that creates the opportunity also attracts more competitors into the category, intensifying rivalry for the same expanding demand.

Colombia's Benchmark Interest Rate

Banco de la República (Colombia's central bank) sets the monetary policy rate that shapes the cost of business credit nationwide. After raising rates by 200 basis points between January and March 2026, and another 75 basis points in July 2026, the Board set the benchmark rate at 12.0%, where it has remained since July 31, 2026.

For our industry, a higher benchmark rate means more expensive financing for equipment purchases, store expansion, or working capital loans — a direct constraint on how quickly Döner House can scale beyond its first location.

Opportunities

  • The rate has held steady at 12.0% since July 31, 2026, giving short-term predictability for budgeting the first store's launch costs.

Threats

  • A benchmark rate 275 basis points higher than at the start of 2026 raises the cost of any future debt-financed expansion beyond the first location.

Availability of Credit

Colombia's gastronomic industry is made up of more than 107,000 establishments, of which 95% are microenterprises. This heavy concentration of small-scale operators directly shapes real financing conditions across the national market.

According to DANE's Micro-Business Survey (EMICRON), access to traditional bank credit to start a food-service operation is extremely limited: only 8.8% of micro-businesses start with a formal bank loan, while the remaining 61.4% depend exclusively on personal savings, own resources, or informal loans from family and acquaintances — a result of limited collateral and low initial banking penetration.

When food-service operators do secure productive credit, the proceeds are typically directed to:

  • Working Capital & Supply — Bulk purchase of raw-material inventory to weather commodity price swings, and payroll coverage during high-variability demand seasons.
  • Delivery & Digital Infrastructure — Specialized packaging, delivery motorcycles, and integration fees with last-mile delivery platforms.
  • Capital Equipment — Industrial stoves, ovens, freezers and cold rooms that increase dispatch volume per square meter.

Opportunities

  • Digital microcredit and fintech lenders evaluate real-time digital cash flow rather than requiring the audited financials traditional banks demand, offering a faster financing path for a new, unproven business.

Threats

  • Only 8.8% of micro-food businesses secure a formal bank loan to start; the 61.4% majority relying on savings or informal credit signals limited institutional support if Döner House needs outside capital.

Inflation Rates

According to DANE's official records, Colombia's annual inflation stood at 6.03%, while the year-to-date variation reached 4.94%. Although the overall Consumer Price Index (CPI) shows moderation versus previous historical peaks, the cost of living continues to face significant pressure from the divisions most directly tied to mass consumption and the food-service chain.

Within the CPI basket, the categories that most directly affect the fast-food industry rank among the fastest-growing nationally: the Restaurants and Hotels division consistently posts the highest annual variation in the country, at 9.53% (with recent peaks near 9.59%) and a 6.75% year-to-date accumulated rate. The Food and Non-Alcoholic Beverages division shows a 5.82% annual variation and a 6.41% year-to-date figure, with sub-categories such as potatoes, processed meats and agricultural derivatives seeing sharp supply swings that directly hit core input costs.

The fact that the Restaurants division leads national inflation shows that the sector has had to raise menu prices to survive rising operating costs. This, however, contracts demand elasticity: local consumers tighten discretionary spending, forcing fast-food operators to compete more aggressively through process and portion optimization to protect market share without sacrificing profitability.

Opportunities

  • As inflation squeezes discretionary spending, cost-conscious consumers are more likely to trade down from pricier full-service restaurants toward efficient, well-run QSR options like Döner House.

Threats

  • Restaurants & Hotels is the fastest-inflating CPI division nationally (9.53% annual), directly raising rent, utility and input costs faster than the general price level.

Taxes Applicable to the Fast Food Industry in Colombia

Sector analysis: fast food in Colombia — Bogotá D.C. as municipal reference, 2026 tax year

1. Introduction to the Variable (Tax Rates)

This report examines a single variable: the tax burden, expressed as statutory tax rates, borne by the fast-food industry in Colombia, treated as a whole — from small owner-operated independents to franchised chains — with Bogotá D.C. as the reference municipality for local taxes. All figures correspond to the 2026 tax year, for which the Unidad de Valor Tributario (UVT) is set at COP $52,374.

The rate that ultimately applies is not a single fixed number; it is shaped by two decisions each operator in the sector makes:

  • 1. Business model — independent vs. franchise — An independent restaurant that sells prepared food is subject to the 8% Impuesto Nacional al Consumo (INC) instead of the 19% value-added tax (IVA). This single distinction is the largest driver of the effective consumption-tax rate.
  • 2. Tax regime — Régimen SIMPLE vs. the Ordinary regime — A small owner-operated shop may elect the Régimen Simple de Tributación (SIMPLE), which folds income tax, ICA and other levies into one consolidated rate, or remain under the Ordinary regime (35% corporate income tax plus separate municipal filings).

Scope and assumptions

  • Sector scope: fast-food establishments in Colombia, from small owner-operated independents to franchised chains.
  • 2026 tax year; 1 UVT = COP $52,374 (DIAN Resolution 000238 of 2025).
  • Rates shown are statutory headline rates, not effective rates after deductions, discounts or credits.
  • Municipal figures are those of Bogotá D.C.; other municipalities set their own rates.

3. Analysis (Opportunities and Threats)

Applying critical judgement to the data above, the tax setting for Colombia's fast-food industry presents clear opportunities but also real threats, several of them sharpened by 2026 changes.

Opportunities

  • Low, predictable SIMPLE rate — At 3.1% of gross in the first bracket, SIMPLE replaces income tax, ICA and Avisos with a single filing.
  • Consumption-tax advantage of the independent model — Selling prepared food attracts 8% INC rather than 19% IVA, so independent fast-food operators are structurally tax-advantaged on the consumption side, and INC is simpler to administer.

Threats

  • Bogotá ICA increase (2026) — Acuerdo Distrital 1019/2025 lifts the Group-3 ICA component to 3%, the single biggest 2026 change. It erodes SIMPLE's advantage and raises the municipal burden for precisely this sector.
  • Revenue-cap cliff — Crossing 100,000 UVT (COP $5,237.4M) forces exit from SIMPLE into the Ordinary regime — 35% income tax plus IVA registration, a steep, discontinuous step-up.

Synthesis

At the level of the whole fast-food industry, Colombia's 2026 tax framework is, on balance, more an opportunity than a threat — though a qualified one. The sector is overwhelmingly composed of small, owner-operated establishments, and it is exactly this majority that the Régimen SIMPLE and the 8% INC are designed to favour: a low, predictable consolidated rate of 3.1%–4.5% of gross income, a single filing in place of several, and a consumption levy roughly eleven percentage points below the 19% IVA. Together these lower both the cost of entry and the compliance burden that most often pushes small food businesses into informality, so the tax setting actively encourages formalisation and new entry across the industry.

The threat is structural rather than immediate, and it concentrates at the top of the size distribution. As a business grows past the 100,000-UVT ceiling (COP $5,237.4M) it is forced out of SIMPLE into the Ordinary regime, where a 35% income tax, mandatory IVA registration and separately assessed municipal levies roughly double the burden. Franchises and larger chains — which sell branded or retail goods and cannot rely on the independent-restaurant INC treatment — sit under this heavier regime from the outset. For the scaling and franchised segment of the industry, therefore, the same framework that rewards small independents becomes a real cost and a disincentive to grow.

The 2026 changes tilt the balance slightly toward the threat side without reversing it: Bogotá's Acuerdo Distrital 1019/2025 lifts the Group-3 ICA component to 3%, raising the municipal burden for precisely the restaurant category that dominates the sector and trimming SIMPLE's headline advantage. On balance, though, the framework remains a net opportunity for the industry as a whole — it is strongly pro-small-business at the wide base of the market, where most fast-food activity sits, and turns into a meaningful threat only at the growth margin defined by the 100,000-UVT cliff and rising municipal rates. The strategic implication for the sector is clear: Colombia's tax design favours a large population of small, formal, independent operators over a smaller number of scaling chains, and firms should plan their growth around that threshold rather than run into it unprepared.
